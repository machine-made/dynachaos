Introduction

The question that runs through Kunihiko Kaneko’s papers from 1982 to 1998 is how a smooth, ordered system becomes turbulent. Consider a system driven by two independent rhythms. When the ratio of their frequencies is a simple fraction, the rhythms lock and the system repeats periodically. Stronger driving widens the locking intervals into wedge-shaped Arnold tongues in the parameter plane. Between the locked states the system winds quasi-periodically on a smooth ring, a torus.

Increase the nonlinearity further and the torus has three possible fates before chaos: it can oscillate (the ring develops wiggles along its length), double (the ring splits into two nested rings, then four, then eight, as in period doubling), or fractalize (the smooth ring crumples at every scale into a wrinkled invariant set prior to the onset of positive Lyapunov exponents). Chaos, an attractor with a positive Lyapunov exponent, comes only past a critical threshold.

On a spatial lattice of coupled chaotic maps, three collective phenomena appear that no single map shows: spatiotemporal intermittency, in which laminar and turbulent patches coexist and move; pattern dynamics, in which the lattice selects spatial patterns that then compete through defect turbulence; and collective chaos, in which the mean field of a globally coupled system has its own low-dimensional chaotic dynamics and the law of large numbers fails.

Kaneko worked through this whole sequence, from locking to collective chaos, in papers published between 1982 and 1998 (Kaneko 1982, 1984c, 1983c, 1984a, 1985, 1990b, 1989b, 1992b), alongside parallel work on circle-map universality (Jensen et al. 1983; Feigenbaum et al. 1982), coupled-oscillator lattices (Waller and Kapral 1984), and the directed-percolation picture of spatiotemporal intermittency (Pomeau 1986; Chaté and Manneville 1988a).

Four equations cover all of the phenomena above. The circle map θn+1=θn+Ω+Ksin⁡(2πθn)(mod⁡1)\begin{equation*} \theta_{n+1} = \theta_n + \Omega + K \sin(2\pi \theta_n) \pmod{1} \end{equation*} captures phase locking and devil’s staircases. The delayed logistic map xn+1=αxn+(1−α)(1−Dyn2),yn+1=xn\begin{equation*} x_{n+1} = \alpha\, x_n + (1 - \alpha)(1 - D\, y_n^2), \qquad y_{n+1} = x_n \end{equation*} captures torus oscillation, doubling, and fractalization. The coupled map lattice (CML) xn+1(i)=f(xn(i))+ε2[g(xn(i+1))+g(xn(i−1))−2g(xn(i))]\begin{equation*} x_{n+1}(i) = f\bigl(x_n(i)\bigr) + \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\bigl[g\bigl(x_n(i\!+\!1)\bigr) + g\bigl(x_n(i\!-\!1)\bigr) - 2g\bigl(x_n(i)\bigr)\bigr] \end{equation*} captures spatiotemporal intermittency and pattern dynamics. The globally coupled map (GCM) xn+1(i)=(1−ε)f(xn(i))+εN∑j=1Nf(xn(j))\begin{equation*} x_{n+1}(i) = (1 - \varepsilon)\, f\bigl(x_n(i)\bigr) + \frac{\varepsilon}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} f\bigl(x_n(j)\bigr) \end{equation*} captures collective chaos and violation of the law of large numbers. All four are discrete maps, the simplest setting in which each phenomenon occurs. Full equations, with all parameters, appear in the body sections.

Sections 2–7 develop these systems in the order of the physical mechanism, not the order of publication: phase instability, amplitude instability, symmetry breaking, high-dimensional extension, spatial extension, and mean-field coupling. Four modern diagnostics, introduced with references below, appear as “diagnostic spotlights” at the end of the mechanism chapters, instead of in a separate methods section.

Two things have changed since these papers were written: available resolution and the diagnostic toolkit. Kaneko’s parameter scans had of order 10210^2 points (for instance, the 100-point scan in Fig. 1 of (Kaneko 1982)) and his time series 10410^4–10510^5 iterations. The devil’s staircase here has 200 000 parameter values and each Lyapunov sweep of the coupled delayed map 500. The four diagnostic spotlights date from after or late within that period: recurrence plots (Eckmann et al., 1987 (Eckmann et al. 1987); Marwan et al., 2007 (Marwan et al. 2007)), the alignment index SALI (Skokos, 2001 (Skokos 2001); Skokos et al., 2007 (Skokos et al. 2007)), permutation entropy (Bandt and Pompe, 2002 (Bandt and Pompe 2002)), and the 0-1 test for chaos (Gottwald and Melbourne, 2004, 2009 (Gottwald and Melbourne 2004, 2009)). Yamagishi and Kaneko (2020) showed chaos on a high-dimensional torus instead of through its collapse, large surveys now score CML behaviour with statistical metrics (Nolan et al. 2026), and the clustering ideas continue in biological and ecological models (Kaneko 1994b, 1997a), most recently in a preprint by Shimada and Kaneko (2026).

This review provides a reproducible atlas across the full model progression. While Kaneko synthesized portions of this work in monographs and reviews (including his 1986 monograph Collapse of Tori and Genesis of Chaos in Dissipative Systems (Kaneko 1986a), surveys of spatiotemporal chaos and pattern dynamics (Kaneko 1989b, 1989c, 1992b), the 1993 edited volume Theory and Applications of Coupled Map Lattices, and the book with Tsuda, Complex Systems: Chaos and Beyond (Kaneko and Tsuda 2001)), those treatments used disparate notations across papers, relied on coarse parameter scans (∼102\sim 10^2 points), and lacked open, automated reproduction scripts. Here, every figure is tied to a single reproducible command in the companion package dynachaos, parameter scans are recomputed at high density (up to 2×1052 \times 10^5 values) so that fine structures can be inspected directly, modern diagnostics are evaluated side by side on identical orbits, and all models are unified under a single consistent notation (Table 1).

Convention: tori in maps versus flows

Throughout this paper, we work exclusively with discrete maps (not flows). A quasi-periodic orbit of a 2D map lies on an invariant circle (topologically 𝕋1\mathbb{T}^1). More generally, a kk-frequency quasi-periodic motion in a continuous-time flow lies on a kk-torus 𝕋k\mathbb{T}^k; its Poincaré return map reduces the torus dimension by one to an invariant (k−1)(k-1)-torus 𝕋k−1\mathbb{T}^{k-1}, because the section removes the neutral direction along the flow. When we say “three-torus” we follow Kaneko’s usage (referring to three-frequency motion in the underlying flow), understanding that the corresponding map carries an invariant 𝕋2\mathbb{T}^2 with two zero Lyapunov exponents, rather than the three zero exponents of the continuous-time flow.

Glossary

Definitions of key terms used throughout the paper.

Arnold tongue

A wedge-shaped region in the two-parameter plane of a periodically forced oscillator within which the oscillator frequency locks to a rational multiple of the driving frequency. Named after V. I. Arnol’d.

Collective chaos

Chaotic dynamics of the macroscopic mean field in a system of many coupled elements, with no sign of decay up to N=20000N = 20\,000 (as tested here).

Devil’s staircase

A monotone function (typically rotation number versus a parameter) consisting of infinitely many rational plateaus (lockings) interspersed with a Cantor set of irrational values.

Directed percolation (DP)

A non-equilibrium phase transition universality class describing the spreading of an “active” state into an “absorbing” state on a lattice.

Farey tree

A hierarchical organisation of rational numbers in which the mediant (p+p′)/(q+q′)(p+p')/(q+q') of two fractions p/qp/q and p′/q′p'/q' appears between them.

Frequency locking

Synchronisation of an oscillator to a rational frequency ratio with an external drive or a coupled partner; the quasi-periodic orbit degenerates into a periodic one.

Lyapunov exponent

The rate of exponential divergence (or convergence) of nearby trajectories. Positive: chaos. Zero: quasi-periodic (marginal) direction. Negative: stable direction.

Neimark–Sacker bifurcation

The discrete-map analogue of a Hopf bifurcation: a fixed point loses stability and an invariant closed curve (torus) is born.

Rotation number

The average angle advanced per iteration on a circle map or invariant circle, ρ=lim⁡n→∞(θn−θ0)/n\rho = \lim_{n\to\infty} (\theta_n - \theta_0)/n.

Strange nonchaotic attractor (SNA)

An attractor with fractal geometry (non-integer dimension) but no positive Lyapunov exponent: geometrically strange, dynamically regular.

Phase Instability: Locking and Staircases

Phase locking transforms continuous quasi-periodic winding into resonant periodic steps, forming a devil’s staircase whose mode-locked plateaus attain full Lebesgue measure at criticality. Kaneko’s 1982 paper (Kaneko 1982) studied period adding in the circle map, and a second paper (Kaneko 1983b) worked out the self-similar scaling of the same sequence. The setting is a single oscillator under a periodic force, and the variable is the drive amplitude: the circle map has no spatial extent, no amplitude dynamics, and no coupling, so only the phase-locking mechanism is left. Kaneko’s computations traced the devil’s staircase and its period-adding sequences, ordered by the Farey tree, in this one-dimensional map. Jensen et al. (1983) and Feigenbaum et al. (1982) analysed the same structure at the same time; Kaneko’s part was the self-similar scaling of the period-adding sequence. We revisit his Ω=0.25\Omega = 0.25 scan from Fig. 1 of (Kaneko 1982) with 200 000 parameter values, where his scan had of the order of a hundred, and add a rotation-number staircase and the Lyapunov exponent.

Circle map and Arnold tongues

The circle map is defined by θn+1=θn+Ω+Ksin⁡(2πθn)(mod⁡1),\begin{equation} \theta_{n+1} = \theta_n + \Omega + K \sin(2\pi \theta_n) \pmod{1}, \label{eq:circle_map} \end{equation} where θn∈[0,1)\theta_n \in [0, 1) lives on the circle 𝕋1\mathbb{T}^1, Ω\Omega controls the bare rotation frequency, and KK controls the nonlinearity. When K=0K = 0, each iteration simply rotates by Ω\Omega: the rotation number ρ=Ω\rho = \Omega is a linear function of the parameter. When K>0K > 0, the nonlinear term distorts this relationship, creating Arnold tongues (Arnol’d 1961): wedge-shaped regions in the (K,Ω)(K, \Omega) plane where the rotation number locks to a rational value p/qp/q.

Figure 2 shows the full tongue structure computed on a 2000×10002000 \times 1000 grid in the (Ω,K)(\Omega, K) plane. Each tongue emanates from a rational Ω=p/q\Omega = p/q at K=0K = 0 and widens as KK increases, with the width proportional to KqK^q for small KK (Arnol’d 1961). The dominant tongues 0/10/1, 1/31/3, 1/21/2, 2/32/3, and 1/11/1 are visible; between them, the Farey-mediant tongues 1/41/4, 2/52/5, 3/7,…3/7, \ldots nest self-similarly. Below the critical line Kc=1/(2π)≈0.1592K_c = 1/(2\pi) \approx 0.1592, the map is a diffeomorphism of the circle and tongues do not overlap: between any two tongues lies a gap of quasi-periodic (irrational ρ\rho) motion. Above KcK_c, tongues overlap, the map ceases to be invertible, and chaotic dynamics are commonly observed in the overlap regions.

Devil’s staircase and period-adding

Kaneko (1982) studied the circle map [eq:circle_map] at fixed rotation frequency Ω=0.25\Omega = 0.25 and swept the nonlinearity KK. The resulting plot (Figure 3): within the pre-chaotic regime (K≲0.182K \lesssim 0.182), ρ(K)\rho(K) is a nonincreasing function with broad rational plateaus (the frequency lockings) separated by quasi-periodic intervals. Although the standard “complete devil’s staircase” is more often plotted as ρ(Ω)\rho(\Omega) at fixed critical KK, Kaneko’s Ω=0.25\Omega = 0.25 scan isolates the same Farey-locking mechanism along a physically important one-parameter path.

Between any two rational plateaus p/qp/q and p′/q′p'/q' lies another plateau at the Farey mediant (p+p′)/(q+q′)(p+p')/(q+q'). This nesting repeats at every scale, which makes the staircase a fractal and is why it is called a devil’s staircase. At the noninvertibility threshold Kc=1/(2π)≈0.1592K_c = 1/(2\pi) \approx 0.1592, mode-locked plateaus cover full Lebesgue measure; the complement is a Cantor set of measure zero (Jensen et al. 1983, 1984). Below KcK_c, the quasi-periodic windows have positive measure; above it, they shrink to zero. Kaneko (1984e) analysed the supercritical behaviour (K>KcK > K_c) of disordered orbits in detail. The universality of this transition was analysed independently by Feigenbaum et al. (1982) and by Rand et al. (Rand et al. 1982) and Ostlund et al. (Ostlund et al. 1983).

For the specific scan at Ω=0.25\Omega = 0.25, the dominant accumulation is the 1/51/5 locking. Starting from ρ=1/4\rho = 1/4 at K=0K = 0 (n=1n = 1), the staircase passes through the period-adding sequence 2/92/9, 3/143/14, 4/194/19, …\ldots, with general term n/(5n−1)n/(5n-1) (Kaneko 1982, 1983b), before the 1/51/5 plateau opens fully. The sequence accumulates at K∞≈0.1567168K_\infty \approx 0.1567168, determined here by bisection (dynachaos run sec02_circle_map) on the lift locking condition θn+5−θn=1\theta_{n+5} - \theta_n = 1 after 10510^5 transient iterates as the parameter where the boundary of the 1/51/5 locking tongue reaches Ω=0.25\Omega = 0.25. Between successive members of this sequence lie narrower Farey-descendant windows, so the local structure still reflects the standard devil’s-staircase hierarchy.

Diagnostic spotlight: rotation number and Lyapunov exponent

Amplitude Instability: Oscillation, Doubling, and Fractalization

When an invariant circle loses amplitude stability independently of its phase, the torus can oscillate, double, or fractalize into a wrinkled nonchaotic set prior to the onset of positive Lyapunov exponents. Torus oscillation entered the literature in “Oscillation and Doubling of Torus” (Kaneko 1984c) as a route to chaos distinct from frequency locking and from period doubling. To see it, one needs a map that carries a smooth invariant circle whose amplitude can go unstable independently of its phase. The delayed logistic map does this with a memory term (yn+1=xny_{n+1} = x_n) in place of explicit time dependence: the torus lives in a two-dimensional state space and its shape can deform continuously. Sweeping the single parameter DD in this one model produces all three fates, oscillation, doubling, and fractalization; the monograph (Kaneko 1986a) treats them together. Here Fig. 1 of (Kaneko 1984c) is recomputed with 100 000 iterates per panel, and a 2000-value Lyapunov sweep, the correlation dimension, and SALI are added.

The delayed logistic map (canonical model)

Kaneko (1984c) studied torus instabilities in their purest form using the two-dimensional delayed logistic map: xn+1=αxn+(1−α)(1−Dyn2),yn+1=xn.\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} x_{n+1} &= \alpha\, x_n + (1 - \alpha)(1 - D\, y_n^2), \\ y_{n+1} &= x_n. \end{aligned} \label{eq:delayed_logistic} \end{equation} Here α∈(0,1)\alpha \in (0, 1) is the delay mixing weight (a convex parameter controlling how much the current value xnx_n carries forward versus the nonlinear driving term) and DD is the drive amplitude. The map has a fixed point x*=y*=(1+4D−1)/(2D)x^* = y^* = (\sqrt{1 + 4D} - 1)/(2D). The Jacobian at the fixed point is J=[α−2(1−α)Dx*10]J = \bigl[\begin{smallmatrix} \alpha & -2(1-\alpha)D\,x^* \\ 1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr] with det⁡J=2(1−α)Dx*\det J = 2(1-\alpha)D\,x^*. A Neimark–Sacker bifurcation occurs when det⁡J=1\det J = 1, giving Dc=3−2α4(1−α)2.\begin{equation} D_c = \frac{3 - 2\alpha}{4(1 - \alpha)^2}. \label{eq:hopf_threshold} \end{equation} At α=0.3\alpha = 0.3, Dc≈1.2245D_c \approx 1.2245. For D>DcD > D_c, the fixed point becomes unstable and a smooth torus (invariant closed curve) appears.

Torus oscillation

As DD increases beyond DcD_c, the torus initially grows smoothly. But at a critical value of DD, the torus begins to oscillate: the closed curve develops wiggles along its length. Kaneko (1984c) traced this to the damped oscillation of the unstable manifold of a periodic saddle near a frequency locking, and read it as an instability along the amplitude direction of the torus, distinct from both frequency locking and torus doubling.

Kaneko describes the progression as DD increases (his Fig. 1 (Kaneko 1984c)): oscillation of the torus, then frequency lockings, then chaos emerging via a locking, then a widening belt-like chaotic attractor, until the unstable fixed point becomes a snap-back repeller. Torus doubling (Maps I and IV)

Period doubling, the cascade 1→2→4→8→⋯1 \to 2 \to 4 \to 8 \to \cdots whose universality Feigenbaum established (Feigenbaum 1978), is the best-known route to chaos in one-dimensional maps. Arnéodo, Coullet, and Arnéodo et al. (1983) discovered that an invariant torus can undergo an analogous cascade of period doublings in continuous-time flows. Kaneko (1983a) then demonstrated that discrete maps can exhibit the same torus-doubling cascade. Just as a fixed point can bifurcate into a period-2 orbit (two alternating points), a smooth invariant circle (𝕋1\mathbb{T}^1 in the map; “torus” in Kaneko’s flow language) can bifurcate into a doubled torus, on which the trajectory alternates between two closed curves on successive iterates. A further doubling produces a 4×4\times-torus, then an 8×8\times-torus, and so on until the tori become so convoluted that they merge into a chaotic attractor.

Kaneko studied four maps exhibiting torus doubling (Kaneko 1983a). Maps (II) and (III) are intermediate cases that interpolate between the three-dimensional Map (I) and the four-dimensional Map (IV); we focus on the two extremes. Map (I) is a three-dimensional delayed logistic: xn+1=αxn+(1−α)(1−Dyn2),yn+1=zn,zn+1=xn,\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} x_{n+1} &= \alpha\, x_n + (1 - \alpha)(1 - D\, y_n^2), \\ y_{n+1} &= z_n, \qquad z_{n+1} = x_n, \end{aligned} \label{eq:map_I} \end{equation} and Map (IV) is a four-dimensional variant: xn+1=αxn+(1−α)(1−Dyn2),yn+1=zn,zn+1=αzn+(1−α)(1−Dwn2),wn+1=xn,\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} x_{n+1} &= \alpha\, x_n + (1 - \alpha)(1 - D\, y_n^2), \\ y_{n+1} &= z_n, \\ z_{n+1} &= \alpha\, z_n + (1 - \alpha)(1 - D\, w_n^2), \\ w_{n+1} &= x_n, \end{aligned} \label{eq:map_IV} \end{equation}

The parameter DD controls how strongly the delayed variable drives the system. As DD increases at fixed α\alpha, the torus undergoes successive doublings: 1×→2×→4×→⋯1\times \to 2\times \to 4\times \to \cdots, accumulating at a critical value D∞D_\infty beyond which the motion is chaotic.

Kaneko found that the doubling cascade obeys a scaling relation analogous to Feigenbaum’s: 2l∝εl−γ,γ≈1/3,\begin{equation} 2^l \propto \varepsilon_l^{-\gamma}, \qquad \gamma \approx 1/3, \label{eq:torus_doubling_scaling} \end{equation} where εl\varepsilon_l is the width of the ll-th doubling tongue. Kaneko reported the exponent γ≈1/3\gamma \approx 1/3 from numerical fits to the first few doublings. We quote this value directly from Kaneko (1983a) rather than remeasuring it. For Map (I) at α=0.4\alpha = 0.4, Kaneko’s Table I (Kaneko 1983a) records a single doubling, at D1=2.151D_1 = 2.151, and the onset of chaos at Dc=2.163D_c = 2.163; the cascade stops after one doubling. A Lyapunov scan over D∈[2.150,2.175]D \in [2.150, 2.175] with 20 000 iterations per point (dynachaos run sec04_doubling) keeps |λ1|<0.002|\lambda_1| < 0.002 throughout, so at this length it does not resolve D1D_1 and DcD_c; the two values are Kaneko’s. Fractalization of torus

At the boundary between quasi-periodic motion and chaos, Kaneko (1984b) found no single breaking event but a continuous process: the torus fractalizes.

On a smooth torus, the attractor is a one-dimensional curve (a closed orbit in the Poincaré section). Its fractal dimension is exactly 1. As the control parameter approaches the chaos threshold, the curve develops wrinkles, fine-scale oscillations that fold it at ever smaller length scales. The fractal dimension increases continuously from 1 toward ∼1.3\sim 1.3–1.51.5 at the onset of chaos.

At this stage, the attractor is a wrinkled invariant set whose maximal Lyapunov exponent has not yet turned positive. Symmetry Breaking: Torus Collapse via Coupling

Coupling identical oscillators introduces exchange symmetries whose breaking produces coexisting out-of-phase attractors and complex, fractal basin boundaries. In the same year as the torus-doubling paper, Kaneko took up a second route to chaos, the transition from torus to chaos accompanied by frequency lockings with symmetry breaking (Kaneko 1983c), in two identical logistic maps coupled symmetrically. The coupled logistic map is the simplest system with two-frequency quasi-periodicity built from identical parts, and the x↔yx \leftrightarrow y exchange symmetry gives it a natural order parameter. The result was that symmetry breaking and basin fractality arise from the topology of coexisting attractors and not from parameter-space bifurcations alone; Takesue and Kaneko (1984) then showed that the basin boundaries are self-similar fractals. We pair a coarse (a,ε)(a,\varepsilon) survey with an attractor sequence at fixed ε\varepsilon; this is an adaptation of Kaneko’s fixed-DD regime diagram. The 0-1 test is added as a model-free chaos diagnostic.

The coupled logistic map (canonical model)

To study two-frequency quasi-periodicity (an invariant circle 𝕋1\mathbb{T}^1 in this 2D return map), Kaneko introduced the coupled logistic map (Kaneko 1983c): xn+1=1−axn2+ε(yn−xn),yn+1=1−ayn2+ε(xn−yn),\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} x_{n+1} &= 1 - a\, x_n^2 + \varepsilon\,(y_n - x_n), \\ y_{n+1} &= 1 - a\, y_n^2 + \varepsilon\,(x_n - y_n), \end{aligned} \label{eq:coupled_logistic} \end{equation} where aa controls the nonlinearity (logistic map parameter) and ε\varepsilon is the coupling strength. This map has an exchange symmetry x↔yx \leftrightarrow y. For ε>0\varepsilon> 0 and moderate aa, the coupling gives rise to a quasi-periodic attractor (invariant circle).

Phase diagram and symmetry-broken attractors

Kaneko showed that as aa increases at fixed ε\varepsilon, the attractor can pass through a sequence of symmetry-preserving and symmetry-broken regimes:

  1. Symmetric regular dynamics: fixed or periodic orbits with x≈yx \approx y.

  2. Locked windows: regular periodic windows embedded in the quasiperiodic route.

  3. Symmetry breaking: the x↔yx \leftrightarrow y symmetry breaks, creating two coexisting attractors related by exchange.

  4. Chaotic bands: irregular sets with positive finite-time expansion rate.

High-Dimensional Extension: Three-Torus and Forced Phase Maps

Higher-dimensional tori collapse into chaos through intermediate resonant lockings rather than direct disintegration, giving rise to multi-frequency double staircases. “Fates of Three-Torus” (Kaneko 1984a) addresses the Ruelle–Takens conjecture that three-frequency quasi-periodicity is structurally unstable. Ruelle and Takens (1971) had argued that 𝕋3\mathbb{T}^3 is generically destroyed by perturbations, and Grebogi et al. (1983) had found numerically that three-frequency orbits are rare. Kaneko’s question was what replaces the torus. A four-dimensional coupled delayed logistic map removes every continuous-time complication and leaves the competition among three frequencies in the simplest discrete setting. In that map he found a double devil’s staircase of lockings, a new fractalization phenomenon, and a transition to chaos that passes through intermediate lockings (𝕋3→𝕋2→\mathbb{T}^3 \to \mathbb{T}^2 \to cycle →\to chaos) and never, in his scans, leaves 𝕋3\mathbb{T}^3 directly. This is a longer list than the stable-or-unstable verdict of Ruelle–Takens. Grebogi et al. (Grebogi et al. 1985) independently studied attractors on NN-tori. We recompute the double staircase and representative (xn,zn)(x_n, z_n) projections at modern resolution.

The mechanisms in Sections 2–4 act in one or two dimensions. The next step adds a third incommensurate frequency.

The coupled delayed logistic map

A three-torus 𝕋3\mathbb{T}^3 is a quasi-periodic attractor with three independent, incommensurate frequencies ω1,ω2,ω3\omega_1, \omega_2, \omega_3. It is the dynamical object that appears in Landau’s route to turbulence (Landau 1944) after three Hopf bifurcations. Ruelle and Takens (1971) argued that 𝕋3\mathbb{T}^3 should be structurally unstable: generic perturbations destroy it and replace it with a strange attractor. Newhouse et al. Double devil’s staircase

Diagnostic spotlight: permutation entropy and complexity-entropy plane

Permutation entropy (Bandt and Pompe 2002) quantifies the complexity of a time series by examining the relative ordering of dd consecutive values. Each window of dd values defines an ordinal pattern (permutation of {0,1,…,d−1}\{0, 1, \ldots, d-1\}). The normalised Shannon entropy of the pattern distribution gives HPE=−1log⁡(d!)∑πpπlog⁡pπ∈[0,1].\begin{equation} H_\text{PE} = -\frac{1}{\log(d!)} \sum_\pi p_\pi \log p_\pi \in [0, 1]. \end{equation}

For an embedding dimension d=5d = 5, a periodic orbit of period pp supports at most pp distinct ordinal permutations among the 5!=1205! = 120 possible patterns. When each pattern appears with equal frequency 1/p1/p, the normalised permutation entropy takes the exact value HPE=ln⁡(p)/ln⁡(5!)H_\text{PE} = \ln(p)/\ln(5!), producing discrete plateaus at HPE≈0.145H_\text{PE} \approx 0.145 for period 2 (p=2p=2), ≈0.290\approx 0.290 for period 4 (p=4p=4), and ≈0.434\approx 0.434 for period 8 (p=8p=8). These plateaus explain the discrete steps visible in Figure 21, rather than an immediate drop to zero. More irregular dynamics populates many patterns, pushing HPEH_\text{PE} upward. Combined with the Jensen–Shannon statistical complexity CJSC_\text{JS} (Rosso et al. 2007), the pair (HPE,CJS)(H_\text{PE}, C_\text{JS}) locates the dynamics in a complexity-entropy plane (Figure 22). Noise occupies the high-HH, low-CC corner; chaos occupies an intermediate region; and periodic motion clusters near the lower boundary.

Spatial Extension: Coupled Map Lattices

Coupling chaotic maps on a spatial lattice produces spatiotemporal intermittency and pattern competition, where local chaos propagates across space with a well-defined speed. The coupled map lattice (Kaneko 1985) followed Kaneko’s study of kink–antikink dynamics (Kaneko 1984d). It is a minimal spatially extended dynamical system: discrete time, discrete space, continuous state, and no partial differential equation. The question it was built for is what changes when many chaotic maps are coupled in space. The answer that mattered most was spatiotemporal intermittency (STI), a coexistence of laminar and bursting regions. Crutchfield and Kaneko (1987) surveyed the broad phenomenology and pattern classification of CMLs, while Bunimovich and Sinai (1988) provided a rigorous statistical-mechanics foundation proving the existence of spacetime chaos for hyperbolic map lattices. In the coupled-map-lattice and globally-coupled-map sections, “turbulent” and “turbulence” mean spatiotemporally disordered, high-dimensional chaotic phases in Kaneko’s sense, not solutions of the Navier–Stokes equations. Waller and Kapral (1984) and Kapral (1985) introduced similar lattices of coupled oscillators independently, and Chaté and Chaté and Manneville (1988a) later studied the STI transition in detail. We recompute Kaneko’s spacetime diagrams on 200 sites for 500 steps, add a spatial activity map on a 200 by 160 parameter grid, and use recurrence quantification analysis as a single-site benchmark.

CML definition and three models

A coupled map lattice (CML) is a discrete-time, discrete-space dynamical system (Kaneko 1985): xn+1(i)=f(xn(i))+ε2[g(xn(i+1))+g(xn(i−1))−2g(xn(i))],\begin{equation} x_{n+1}(i) = f\bigl(x_n(i)\bigr) + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Bigl[g\bigl(x_n(i+1)\bigr) + g\bigl(x_n(i-1)\bigr) - 2\, g\bigl(x_n(i)\bigr)\Bigr], \label{eq:cml} \end{equation} where i=1,…,Ni = 1, \ldots, N are lattice sites with periodic boundary conditions, f(x)f(x) is the local map, g(x)g(x) is the coupling function, and ε\varepsilon is the coupling strength. The diffusive coupling on the right-hand side is the discrete Laplacian Δg=gi+1+gi−1−2gi\Delta g = g_{i+1} + g_{i-1} - 2g_i, the simplest model of nearest-neighbour interaction.

Kaneko studied three CML models with different local dynamics:

  1. A piecewise map, f(x)=x+x2+af(x) = x + x^2 + a for x<cx < c and f(x)=−3(x−c)+1+af(x) = -3(x - c) + 1 + a for x≥cx \ge c, with c=(5−1)/2c = (\sqrt{5}-1)/2, a=−0.01a = -0.01 and g=fg = f.

  2. Coupled circle maps with f(θ)=θ+Ksin⁡(2πθ)+Ωf(\theta) = \theta + K\sin(2\pi\theta) + \Omega and g(θ)=sin⁡(2πθ)g(\theta) = \sin(2\pi\theta).

  3. Coupled logistic maps with f(x)=1−ax2f(x) = 1 - ax^2 and g=fg = f.

Spatiotemporal intermittency and directed percolation

All three models exhibit spatiotemporal intermittency (STI): the spacetime diagram (Figure 23) shows alternating laminar (ordered) and burst (disordered) regions. As the coupling ε\varepsilon is tuned, the fraction of bursts changes continuously, analogous to a phase transition.

Kaneko reduced the laminar/burst dynamics to a binary (cellular automaton) description (Kaneko 1985) and characterised the transition through burst-propagation speed vB∝(ε−εc)νv_B \propto (\varepsilon- \varepsilon_c)^\nu with ν≈0.6\nu \approx 0.6–0.90.9, and the growth of positive Lyapunov exponents. Pomeau (1986) suggested a connection to the universality class of directed percolation (DP), the standard absorbing-state transition of non-equilibrium statistical mechanics (Hinrichsen 2000). Chaté and Chaté and Manneville (1988a, 1988b) investigated this connection and found DP-like scaling features in deterministic spatiotemporal intermittency, while noting that the critical exponents may depend on whether the laminar state is strictly absorbing (Chaté and Manneville 1989b, 1989a); Kaneko himself noted that “the universality class on critical exponents seems to be different” (Kaneko 1989b).

To complement the qualitative spacetime view with quantitative measures, we extract an STI “spine” from the Kaneko Model-A CML (dynachaos run sec12_intermittency; Figure 24): the turbulent fraction ϕturb\phi_\text{turb} as a function of the coupling ε\varepsilon, together with the laminar-domain size distribution, where an active site is defined by the local burst departure threshold |xn(i+1)−xn(i)|>0.05|x_n(i+1) - x_n(i)| > 0.05. The turbulent fraction is zero up to ε=0.050\varepsilon= 0.050, rises between ε=0.050\varepsilon= 0.050 and 0.0650.065, and reaches a burst-dominated plateau ϕturb≈0.88\phi_\text{turb} \approx 0.88 for ε≥0.07\varepsilon\ge 0.07. At ε=0.050\varepsilon= 0.050 the whole lattice is laminar, so there is no laminar-length distribution. At ε=0.060\varepsilon= 0.060 the distribution is broad (lengths up to 50 sites); among a power law, an exponential and a truncated power law, the power law has the lowest BIC (α≈1.16\alpha \approx 1.16), but the Kolmogorov–Smirnov distances, evaluated at each integer length, reject all three: 0.074 for the power law and the truncated power law and 0.14 for the exponential, against a 5 % critical value of 1.36/n≈0.0101.36/\sqrt{n} \approx 0.010 for n≈1.7×104n \approx 1.7 \times 10^4 lengths (the critical value assumes independent lengths). Testing whether the system falls in the directed percolation (DP) class requires finite-size scaling at the critical coupling εc\varepsilon_c, which is not performed here. Critical exponents of directed percolation have been measured in coupled circle-map lattices (Janaki et al. 2003; Jabeen and Gupte 2005, 2006) and in a ferrofluid experiment (Rupp et al. 2003). What remains open is a rigorous mathematical proof and whether the Model-A lattice studied here belongs to the DP universality class.

Pattern dynamics (five phases)

Kaneko (1989b) systematically classified the dynamics of the CML [eq:cml] with the logistic map f(x)=1−ax2f(x) = 1 - ax^2 and g=fg = f. The canonical taxonomy in the (a,ε)(a, \varepsilon) plane contains five phases:

  1. Frozen random (a≲1.5a \lesssim 1.5, moderate ε\varepsilon): the lattice settles into a static, spatially disordered pattern.

  2. Pattern selection (a≈1.6a \approx 1.6–1.71.7): the lattice spontaneously selects a spatially periodic pattern, breaking translational symmetry.

  3. Defect turbulence (a≈1.7a \approx 1.7–1.81.8): the selected pattern is disrupted by defects (domain walls) that nucleate, propagate, and annihilate chaotically.

  4. Pattern competition intermittency (a≈1.8a \approx 1.8–1.91.9): large coherent domains compete for territory, punctuated by intermittent bursts of turbulence.

  5. Fully developed turbulence (a≳1.9a \gtrsim 1.9): all spatial coherence is lost.

Thermodynamics of spatiotemporal chaos

For a CML with NN sites, the full Lyapunov spectrum {λi}i=1N\{\lambda_i\}_{i=1}^{N} grows with system size. Kaneko (1989d) introduced the concept of intensive thermodynamic quantities for spatiotemporal chaos: the KS entropy density hKS=HKS/Nh_\text{KS} = H_\text{KS}/N, the Lyapunov dimension density dKY/Nd_\text{KY}/N, and the density of positive Lyapunov exponents. When the spatial correlation length ξ\xi is much smaller than the system size NN, these densities converge to well-defined intensive values, and the CML behaves like a thermodynamic system with local degrees of freedom.

This convergence breaks down near phase transitions (where ξ→∞\xi \to \infty) and in the pattern selection phase (where long-range spatial order imposes correlations across the lattice). Diagnostic spotlight: RQA and spacetime recurrence

Recurrence plots (Eckmann et al. 1987; Marwan et al. 2007) visualise the times at which a trajectory returns to a previously visited neighbourhood. From the binary recurrence matrix Rij=Θ(δ−∥𝒙i−𝒙j∥)R_{ij} = \Theta(\delta - \|\bm{x}_i - \bm{x}_j\|) (where δ\delta is the recurrence threshold), one extracts quantitative measures:

  • RR (recurrence rate): fraction of recurrent points.

  • DET (determinism): fraction in diagonal lines (≥lmin\ge l_\text{min}).

  • LAM (laminarity): fraction in vertical lines (≥vmin\ge v_\text{min}).

  • ENTR: Shannon entropy of the diagonal line length distribution.

RQA tracks recurrent states and temporal structures: the laminarity measure LAM tracks the fraction of near-recurrent states, while ENTR captures the structural complexity of diagonal recurrence lines. We evaluate these measures on the single-site delayed logistic map benchmark (Section 3.1) rather than across the full CML lattice, avoiding embedding-dimension ambiguities. Figure 29 shows these four measures versus DD. The transition from torus to chaos manifests as a jump in ENTR and a restructuring of DET and LAM.

Diagnostic spotlight: the temporal-intermittency taxonomy

The laminar/burst dichotomy that organises spatiotemporal intermittency has a classical low-dimensional counterpart. Pomeau and Manneville (1980) classified the intermittent routes to chaos by the way a periodic orbit loses stability: Type I through a tangent (saddle–node) bifurcation, Type II through a subcritical Hopf bifurcation, and Type III through a subharmonic (inverse period-doubling) bifurcation. A distinct mechanism, on–off intermittency, arises when an invariant subspace loses transverse stability at a blowout bifurcation (Platt et al. 1993; Heagy et al. 1994). We apply laminar-phase detection and heavy-tail estimators (maximum-likelihood exponent with bootstrap confidence interval and a goodness-of-fit pp-value) to canonical realisations of each route, as a check that the diagnostics behave correctly where the underlying scaling laws are known.

Type I (Figure 30) is realised by the logistic map just below its period-3 window, where three tangent channels open between the third iterate and the diagonal. The laminar-length distribution follows a power law (α≈−1.5\alpha \approx -1.5, goodness-of-fit p=0.45p = 0.45) and the mean laminar length obeys the normal-form scaling ⟨ℓ⟩∝ε−1/2\langle \ell \rangle \propto \varepsilon^{-1/2}; the Lorenz system at ρ=166.2\rho = 166.2 provides a continuous-time exemplar of the same near-diagonal reinjection channel.

These temporal routes give the spatiotemporal case a vocabulary, not a decomposition. Each laminar interval in the coupled-map-lattice intermittency of Section 6.2 is, locally in time, a site lingering near a marginally stable state before a burst, and the classical types list the ways such local stability can be lost. No single type accounts for the full lattice dynamics, which couple these local events across space.

Mean-Field Extension: Globally Coupled Maps

Global all-to-all coupling enables macroscopic collective chaos and spontaneous dynamical clustering, causing mean-field fluctuations to persist in the thermodynamic limit. The globally coupled map (Kaneko 1990b, 1989a) replaces the nearest-neighbour diffusion of the CML with all-to-all coupling, its mean-field limit. With no spatial structure left, what remains is the relation between chaos in each element and order in the whole, and the question Kaneko put to it was whether a system can be chaotic element by element and still fail to self-average. In parallel work on globally coupled limit-cycle oscillators, Nakagawa and Kuramoto (1993) demonstrated that a population of chaotic elements can exhibit low-dimensional collective chaos in its macroscopic order parameter. Pikovsky and Kurths (1994) subsequently examined the statistical properties of globally coupled map ensembles, investigating whether the apparent violation of the law of large numbers persists in the infinite-size limit. Three phenomena emerged that a nearest-neighbour lattice cannot show: dynamical clustering (symmetry breaking among identical elements), a failure of the law of large numbers (the mean field keeps finite fluctuations as N→∞N \to \infty), and collective chaos (the mean field has its own low-dimensional chaotic dynamics). Clustering was developed further in (Kaneko 1990a, 1991, 1992a). We recompute Kaneko’s MSD-versus-NN plot (Fig. 1 of (Kaneko 1990b)) and add figures of cluster states, of the largest Lyapunov exponent, and a reproduction of the collective Lyapunov exponent of Shibata and Kaneko (1998). Kaneko (1990b) introduced the globally coupled map (GCM): xn+1(i)=(1−ε)f(xn(i))+εN∑j=1Nf(xn(j)),\begin{equation} x_{n+1}(i) = (1 - \varepsilon)\, f\bigl(x_n(i)\bigr) + \frac{\varepsilon}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} f\bigl(x_n(j)\bigr), \label{eq:gcm} \end{equation} where the mean field hn=N−1∑jf(xn(j))h_n = N^{-1} \sum_j f(x_n(j)) couples every site to every other.

Collective chaos and cluster states

The nontrivial mean-field dynamics indicates that the NN oscillators, although not synchronised, maintain correlations that resist averaging. Kaneko showed that the system self-organises into cluster states: groups of oscillators that share the same trajectory, with the number and sizes of clusters depending on aa and ε\varepsilon. These clusters are not imposed by the coupling topology (which is all-to-all and thus featureless) but emerge from the dynamics, a form of dynamical symmetry breaking.

Clustered attractors and switching

In the GCM, a cluster state is a partition of the NN sites into kk groups with nearly identical instantaneous values at a given iterate. The partition is characterised by the cluster multiplicities (N1,N2,…,Nk)(N_1, N_2, \ldots, N_k) with ∑lNl=N\sum_l N_l = N. Kaneko (1990a) showed that at moderate coupling (ε≈0.1\varepsilon\approx 0.1), the GCM spontaneously selects a small number of clusters (k=2k = 2–55), and that the partition coding provides a discrete “label” for each macroscopic state.

In two-cluster attractors, coded by the populations N+N_+ and N−N_- of the two clusters, Kaneko found posi-nega switching (Kaneko 1989a): an input on a single site moves it from one cluster to the other, so the system switches among the coded attractors. When N+N_+ is pushed beyond a threshold NthrN_\mathrm{thr}, the system passes through a chaotic transient and every ++ site changes to −- and vice versa. The partially ordered (PO) phase, between full synchronisation and full turbulence, is characterised by clusters that form, merge, and split in a marginally stable regime. Kaneko (1994a) showed that in this regime, information cascades through the cluster hierarchy with marginal stability: the system sits at the edge of a bifurcation, generating long-range temporal correlations. Discussion

Kaneko’s papers of 1982–1998 established the vocabulary and the phenomenology of torus dynamics and spatiotemporal chaos. Ordered by the six mechanisms of the Introduction, from phase instability to mean-field coupling, they read as a sequence of steps in complexity instead of a chronology. Recomputing these models reveals both the structural continuity of the transitions and the methodological boundaries of modern diagnostics, such as the behaviour of SALI on dissipative maps, where its decay alone separates a torus from partial locking or chaos but not partial locking from chaos.

This ordering matters because the same operation recurs across different models: add one structural degree of freedom, then ask which invariant object loses stability and which observable records the loss. In the circle map the relevant observable is a rotation number; in torus oscillation and doubling it is the Lyapunov spectrum and the geometry of an invariant circle; in the CML it is the propagation of perturbations through space; in the GCM it is the persistence of mean-field fluctuations as NN grows. The atlas complements broad CML metric surveys such as Nolan et al. (Nolan et al. 2026), which sample many systems and metrics but do not retrace Kaneko’s sequence of mechanisms and figures.

Mechanistic interpretation

The mechanism-based ordering also explains why the same diagnostic cannot carry the whole paper. The appropriate observable is fixed by the invariant object being destabilised. In the phase-locking sections, that object is an invariant circle with a well-defined winding rate, so the rotation number is the natural observable. For torus oscillation and doubling, the change is not only in frequency content but in the stability of transverse directions, which makes the Lyapunov spectrum decisive. The same logic fixes the remaining pairings: basin and order-parameter views for symmetry breaking, where exchange-related attractors coexist; spatial diagnostics for CMLs, where perturbations propagate through laminar and burst regions; and finite-size and mean-field statistics for GCMs, where persistent correlations among nominally identical elements survive averaging.

Higher resolution makes these distinctions visible. Narrow windows resolve locking transitions organised by rational approximants, long trajectories reduce misclassification of weak chaos and nearly neutral tori, and finite-size sweeps test whether collective chaos persists as mean-field averaging becomes more effective. A diagnostic is decisive only when the degree of freedom that goes unstable is one it can see.

Toric chaos

Yamagishi and Kaneko (2020) showed that chaos can exist on a high-dimensional torus and not only through its collapse. In systems of globally coupled phase oscillators, the attractor can be a high-dimensional torus 𝕋k\mathbb{T}^k (with k≫1k \gg 1) on which the dynamics is chaotic, which they call “toric chaos.” This adds a fate to Kaneko’s list for 𝕋3\mathbb{T}^3 and beyond: a higher-dimensional torus can carry chaos without collapsing.

The coupled delayed logistic map (Section 5.1) supports three-torus motion that collapses to chaos as DBD_B increases at fixed coupling (Figure 17). At the transition, two Lyapunov exponents are near zero while one becomes positive. This is the classical torus collapse, not toric chaos in the sense of Yamagishi and Kaneko (2020), which requires the torus to persist as a manifold even while the on-torus dynamics is chaotic.

Biological relevance

Kaneko extended the dynamical clustering framework to biological networks. Kaneko (1994b) showed that the partition structure of GCM cluster states provides a natural encoding of cell-type diversity. In coupled maps with growth and death dynamics, clusters of oscillators that share a common time trace can then be reinterpreted as cell types, with cluster formation playing the role of cell differentiation (Kaneko 1997a). Ecological applications of coupled logistic maps appeared early (Satoh and Aihara 1990; Satoh 1991). In a recent preprint, Shimada and Kaneko (2026) continue this direction by connecting high-dimensional chaos and intermittent switching to coexistence in cross-feeding microbial-community models. In these models the macroscopic state is carried by partitions that form dynamically, not by fixed species labels or imposed network modules, which is why the mean-field and clustering mechanisms are still in use.

Diagnostic synergies

The four modern diagnostics distributed across the preceding sections provide complementary views of Kaneko’s transitions:

  • The 0-1 test (Section 4) is the most practical for binary classification (chaos vs. regular) from scalar data because it does not require equations or tangent maps.

  • SALI (Section 3) requires the linearised flow; in the dissipative maps examined here, phase-space contraction aligns deviation vectors, so SALI stays bounded on a torus and decays on partial locking and on chaos, and its decay alone does not separate those two (cf. Moges et al. (Moges et al. 2025)); the decay rate λ2−λ1\lambda_2 - \lambda_1 does.

  • Permutation entropy (Section 5) works from the ordinal patterns of a scalar observable alone, tracking regular windows and chaotic bands without a Jacobian.

  • RQA (Section 6) responds to laminar residence times and repeated local patterns; we test it here on the single-site torus-to-chaos transition.

These diagnostics are not interchangeable. Each responds to a different way in which order is lost, and the limit of SALI on the dissipative maps above is one reason a single diagnostic cannot carry the paper.

Computational limitations: then and now

Kaneko’s original parameter scans typically used ∼100\sim 100 points with ∼50,000\sim 50{,}000 iterations per point. Our scans use from 500 (Figure 17) to 200 000 (Figure 3) parameter values (Table 4). This increase matters because several transitions are organised by narrow windows, thin strands, or fractal boundaries; at high resolution, the tongue boundaries, period-adding plateaus, and basin stripes can be read directly off the plot.

What persisted after the 1990s

Three later lines of work extended the framework beyond spatiotemporal chaos. First, the collective chaos discovered in the GCM led to a systematic study of Milnor attractor networks (Kaneko 1997b, 1998): in systems with many coexisting attractors, noise-induced transitions are governed by the Milnor attractor structure and not by basin volume. Second, chaotic itinerancy, a slow, directed wandering among partially stable attractor ruins proposed by Tsuda (1991) and developed with Kaneko (1992b, 1995; Kaneko and Tsuda 2001), became an organising idea for high-dimensional chaos. Third, dynamic clustering was reinterpreted as a model of cell differentiation: coupled maps with growth and death dynamics produce spontaneous diversification of cell types (Kaneko 1997a). Separately, renormalisation-group analysis clarified the scaling structure of torus doubling and strange nonchaotic attractors (Kuznetsov et al. 1995; Kuznetsov et al. 1998; Nishikawa and Kaneko 1996).

Open questions

Several questions raised by Kaneko’s work remain open:

  1. Is the torus-doubling exponent γ≈1/3\gamma \approx 1/3 truly universal? Kaneko’s numerical evidence is suggestive but not definitive. Partial renormalisation-group results exist for the torus-doubling terminal point and for quasi-periodically forced one-dimensional maps (Kuznetsov et al. 1998; Jorba et al. 2015, 2016), but a complete renormalisation-group analysis analogous to Feigenbaum’s for period doubling has not been achieved. A fixed point of the doubling operator, with its eigenvalue compared against the fitted value, would settle it.

  2. What is the complete classification of 𝕋3\mathbb{T}^3 fates? Kaneko identified locking, fractalization, and chaos, but the measure-theoretic structure of the phase diagram (which fates are generic, which are exceptional) is not fully understood; the exponential rarity of high-dimensional tori found by Yamagishi and Kaneko (2020) is partial progress. A measure of the locked, fractalized, and chaotic regions as the grid is refined, with a convergence test, would say which fates are generic.

  3. Can the directed percolation universality of STI be proven rigorously? Critical exponents of directed percolation have been measured in coupled circle-map lattices (Janaki et al. 2003; Jabeen and Gupte 2005, 2006) and in experiment (Rupp et al. 2003). What remains open is a rigorous mathematical proof, and whether the Model-A lattice studied here belongs to the DP universality class. Testing the DP class for this lattice requires systematic finite-size scaling of the turbulent fraction across system sizes from 256 to 4096 sites at the critical coupling εc\varepsilon_c, comparing measured exponents against DP bounds.

  4. Does chaos sustain biological diversity? Kaneko’s clustering models of cell differentiation (Kaneko 1994b, 1997a) and ecological applications (Satoh and Aihara 1990; Satoh 1991) connect the two fields. Shimada and Kaneko in a recent preprint (Shimada and Kaneko 2026) ask a concrete form of the question: whether the number of coexisting species exceeds the competitive-exclusion bound in the high-dimensional chaotic regime. The test is a count of coexisting species against that bound in the same model with and without chaos.

Conclusions

Kaneko’s papers of 1982–1998 have been recomputed and arranged by mechanism. Each of the six chapters adds one structural degree of freedom to the one before.

Organising the work this way makes a recurring construction easy to see: a low-dimensional invariant object is destabilised, then a new degree of freedom (amplitude, symmetry, spatial propagation, or mean-field correlation) becomes dynamically active. This pattern was already present in Kaneko’s original papers; recomputing the figures at higher resolution and viewing the models side by side brings it into a single view.

The principal contributions of this review are:

  1. A reproducible figure-to-command atlas recomputing Kaneko’s 1982–1998 models across six physical mechanisms from public open-source code (Table 2).

  2. Denser parameter scans (up to 2×1052 \times 10^5 values) showing the tongue boundaries, period-adding plateaus, and basin stripes at a resolution where they can be read off the plot.

  3. Side-by-side evaluation of modern diagnostics (the 0-1 test, permutation entropy, and recurrence quantification analysis) on canonical benchmark models, identifying their operating domains and documenting that on a dissipative map SALI stays bounded on a torus and decays on partial locking and on chaos, where only its decay rate separates the two.

  4. An open, scripted analysis package (dynachaos) providing automated command-line pipelines for every figure.

The atlas is a reference point: old and new computations of the same models can be compared directly. It establishes no new scaling law, does not replace finite-size scaling for STI, and does not turn the diagnostic spotlights into validated automatic classifiers. Next steps are a controlled finite-size-scaling study of the STI examples, an uncertainty estimate for the torus-doubling exponent, and a comparison between the spotlights used here and larger metric suites for CMLs.

The dynachaos analysis codebase and figure artifacts for this paper are available at https://github.com/openfluids/dynachaos (Apache-2.0); the commit that produced each figure is recorded in the repository history. Reproduction uses published dynachaos entry points rather than private scripts: section figures are regenerated with dynachaos run <section_id> or dynachaos run all, while external scalar/reduced signals are analysed with dynachaos analyze <config.jsonc>. The documented example command dynachaos analyze workflow_fixture.jsonc reads the bundled fixture config and writes workflow_output/results.json plus metadata and summary files; local long-signal analyses follow the same config-to-results pathway.

Assumptions and Validity Checklist

  • Discrete-time maps: all models are nondimensional iterated maps; “time” is iteration count nn.

  • Rotation numbers: computed via the lift (unwrapped phase); near-critical convergence is verified by doubling the averaging length.

  • Lyapunov spectra: computed via QR/Gram–Schmidt re-orthonormalization after a discarded transient; reported curves are stable under doubling the averaging length.

  • CML boundary conditions: periodic in space unless stated; diagnostics sampling along a ray (e.g. λ(v)\lambda(v)) use windows short enough to avoid wrap-around artifacts (see Sec. 6.4).

  • Fractal dimension estimates: correlation dimension D2D_2 depends on finite sampling (MM points), temporal correlations (mitigated by the Theiler window (Theiler 1986)), and scaling-region selection; the ±1σ\pm1\sigma fit uncertainty on D2D_2 is reported in Figure 11.

  • Universality-class language (e.g. “DP-like”): treated as conjectural unless supported by finite-size scaling with uncertainty estimates.
