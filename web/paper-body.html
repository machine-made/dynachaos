Introduction

The question that runs through Kunihiko Kaneko’s papers from 1982 to 1998 is how a smooth, ordered system becomes turbulent. Consider a system driven by two independent rhythms. When the ratio of their frequencies is a simple fraction, the rhythms lock and the system repeats periodically. Stronger driving widens the locking intervals into wedge-shaped Arnold tongues in the parameter plane. Between the locked states the system winds quasi-periodically on a smooth ring, a torus.

Increase the nonlinearity further and the torus has three possible fates before chaos. It can oscillate (the ring wobbles and spectral sidebands appear), double (the ring splits into two nested rings, then four, then eight, as in period doubling), or fractalize (the smooth ring crumples at every scale into a fractal set that is geometrically strange but not yet chaotic). Chaos, an attractor with a positive Lyapunov exponent, comes only past a critical threshold.

On a spatial lattice of coupled chaotic maps, three collective phenomena appear that no single map shows: spatiotemporal intermittency, in which laminar and turbulent patches coexist and move; pattern dynamics, in which the lattice selects spatial patterns that then compete through defect turbulence; and collective chaos, in which the mean field of a globally coupled system has its own low-dimensional chaotic dynamics and the law of large numbers fails.

Kaneko worked through this whole sequence, from locking to collective chaos, in papers published between 1982 and 1998 (Kaneko 1992b, 1997b, 1998), alongside parallel work on circle-map universality (Jensen et al. 1983; Feigenbaum et al. 1982), coupled-oscillator lattices (Waller and Kapral 1984), and the directed-percolation picture of spatiotemporal intermittency (Pomeau 1986; Chaté and Manneville 1988a).

Four equations cover all of the phenomena above. The circle map θn+1=θn+Ω+Ksin⁡(2πθn)(mod⁡1)\begin{equation*} \theta_{n+1} = \theta_n + \Omega + K \sin(2\pi \theta_n) \pmod{1} \end{equation*} captures phase locking and devil’s staircases. The delayed logistic map xn+1=αxn+(1−α)(1−Dyn2),yn+1=xn\begin{equation*} x_{n+1} = \alpha\, x_n + (1 - \alpha)(1 - D\, y_n^2), \qquad y_{n+1} = x_n \end{equation*} captures torus oscillation, doubling, and fractalization. The coupled map lattice (CML) xn+1(i)=f(xn(i))+ε2[f(xn(i+1))+f(xn(i−1))−2f(xn(i))]\begin{equation*} x_{n+1}(i) = f\bigl(x_n(i)\bigr) + \tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\bigl[f\bigl(x_n(i\!+\!1)\bigr) + f\bigl(x_n(i\!-\!1)\bigr) - 2f\bigl(x_n(i)\bigr)\bigr] \end{equation*} captures spatiotemporal intermittency and pattern dynamics. The globally coupled map (GCM) xn+1(i)=(1−ε)f(xn(i))+εN∑j=1Nf(xn(j))\begin{equation*} x_{n+1}(i) = (1 - \varepsilon)\, f\bigl(x_n(i)\bigr) + \frac{\varepsilon}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} f\bigl(x_n(j)\bigr) \end{equation*} captures collective chaos and violation of the law of large numbers. All four are discrete maps, the simplest setting in which each phenomenon occurs. Full equations, with all parameters, appear in the body sections.

Sections 2–7 develop these systems in the order of the physical mechanism, not the order of publication: phase instability, amplitude instability, symmetry breaking, high-dimensional extension, spatial extension, and mean-field coupling. Four modern diagnostics, introduced with references below, appear as “diagnostic spotlights” at the end of the mechanism chapters, instead of in a separate methods section.

Two things have changed since these papers were written: computing power and the diagnostics. Kaneko’s parameter scans had of order 10210^2 points and his time series 10410^4–10510^5 iterations. A laptop recomputes every figure overnight; the devil’s staircase here has 200 000 parameter values and each Lyapunov sweep of the coupled delayed map 500. The diagnostics that did not exist then are the 0-1 test for chaos (Gottwald and Melbourne 2004), the SALI/GALI alignment indices (Skokos 2001; Skokos et al. 2007), permutation entropy (Bandt and Pompe 2002), and recurrence quantification analysis (Marwan et al. 2007). Yamagishi and Kaneko (2020) showed chaos on a high-dimensional torus instead of through its collapse, large surveys now score CML behaviour with statistical metrics (Nolan et al. 2026), and the clustering ideas continue in biological and ecological models (Kaneko 1994b, 1997a), most recently in a preprint by Shimada and Kaneko (2026). This paper adds a reproducible atlas. It contains no new route to chaos, no new diagnostic, and no complete phase diagram of any one model. It recomputes Kaneko’s figures from public code at a resolution where the relations among the models can be read off the plots, and cross-checks them with the diagnostics above. The dynachaos package that produces every figure is general: scripted dynamical-systems models, time-series diagnostics, and figure pipelines whose inputs are recorded. This paper is its first full application.

Convention: tori in maps versus flows

Throughout this paper, we work exclusively with discrete maps (not flows). A quasi-periodic orbit of a 2D map lies on an invariant circle (topologically 𝕋1\mathbb{T}^1). A kk-frequency quasi-periodic motion in a continuous-time flow lies on a kk-torus; its Poincaré return map reduces this to an invariant (k−1)(k-1)-torus, because the section removes one neutral direction. When we say “three-torus” we follow Kaneko’s usage (referring to three-frequency motion in the underlying flow), understanding that the corresponding map has a 𝕋2\mathbb{T}^2 invariant set with two zero Lyapunov exponents. In a flow, the same three-frequency motion would show three zero exponents (the third being along the flow direction).

More precisely, for a quasi-periodic orbit on 𝕋k\mathbb{T}^k in a flow, the Poincaré return map reduces the torus dimension by one: three incommensurate frequencies in the flow become two zero Lyapunov exponents in the map. The terms “kk-torus” and “kk-frequency quasi-periodicity” both describe the flow; the map carries the (k−1)(k-1)-torus.

Phase Instability: Locking and Staircases

Kaneko’s 1982 paper (Kaneko 1982) studied period adding in the circle map, and a second paper (Kaneko 1983b) worked out the self-similar scaling of the same sequence. The setting is a single oscillator under a periodic force, and the variable is the drive amplitude: the circle map has no spatial extent, no amplitude dynamics, and no coupling, so only the phase-locking mechanism is left. Kaneko’s computations traced the devil’s staircase and its period-adding sequences, ordered by the Farey tree, in this one-dimensional map. Jensen, Bak, and Bohr (Jensen et al. 1983) and Feigenbaum, Kadanoff, and Shenker (Feigenbaum et al. 1982) analysed the same structure at the same time; Kaneko’s part was the self-similar scaling of the period-adding sequence. We revisit his Ω=0.25\Omega = 0.25 scan from Fig. 1 of (Kaneko 1982) with 200 000 parameter values, where his scan had of the order of a hundred, and add a rotation-number staircase and the Lyapunov exponent.

Circle map and Arnold tongues

The circle map is defined by θn+1=θn+Ω+Ksin⁡(2πθn)(mod⁡1),\begin{equation} \theta_{n+1} = \theta_n + \Omega + K \sin(2\pi \theta_n) \pmod{1}, \label{eq:circle_map} \end{equation} where θn∈[0,1)\theta_n \in [0, 1) lives on the circle 𝕋1\mathbb{T}^1, Ω\Omega controls the bare rotation frequency, and KK controls the nonlinearity. When K=0K = 0, each iteration simply rotates by Ω\Omega: the rotation number ρ=Ω\rho = \Omega is a linear function of the parameter. When K>0K > 0, the nonlinear term distorts this relationship, creating Arnold tongues (Arnol’d 1961): wedge-shaped regions in the (K,Ω)(K, \Omega) plane where the rotation number locks to a rational value p/qp/q.

Figure 2 shows the full tongue structure computed on a 2000×10002000 \times 1000 grid in the (Ω,K)(\Omega, K) plane. Each tongue emanates from a rational Ω=p/q\Omega = p/q at K=0K = 0 and widens as KK increases, with the width proportional to KqK^q for small KK (Arnol’d 1961). The dominant tongues 0/10/1, 1/31/3, 1/21/2, 2/32/3, and 1/11/1 are clearly visible; between them, the Farey-mediant tongues 1/41/4, 2/52/5, 3/7,…3/7, \ldots nest self-similarly. Below the critical line Kc=1/(2π)≈0.1592K_c = 1/(2\pi) \approx 0.1592, the map is a diffeomorphism of the circle and tongues do not overlap: between any two tongues lies a gap of quasi-periodic (irrational ρ\rho) motion. Above KcK_c, tongues overlap, the map ceases to be invertible, and chaotic dynamics are commonly observed in the overlap regions.

Devil’s staircase and period-adding

Kaneko (1982) studied the circle map [eq:circle_map] at fixed rotation frequency Ω=0.25\Omega = 0.25 and swept the nonlinearity KK. The resulting plot of ρ\rho versus KK is a staircase-like locking curve (Figure 3): a nonincreasing function with broad rational plateaus (the frequency lockings) separated by quasi-periodic or chaotic intervals. Although the standard “complete devil’s staircase” is more often plotted as ρ(Ω)\rho(\Omega) at fixed critical KK, Kaneko’s Ω=0.25\Omega = 0.25 scan isolates the same Farey-locking mechanism along a physically important one-parameter path.

Between any two rational plateaus p/qp/q and p′/q′p'/q' lies another plateau at the Farey mediant (p+p′)/(q+q′)(p+p')/(q+q'). This nesting repeats at every scale, which makes the staircase a fractal and is why it is called a devil’s staircase. At the noninvertibility threshold Kc=1/(2π)≈0.1592K_c = 1/(2\pi) \approx 0.1592, mode-locked plateaus cover the full Ω\Omega-axis; the remaining set of quasi-periodic rotation numbers is Cantor-like (Jensen et al. 1983, 1984). Below KcK_c, the quasi-periodic windows have positive measure; above it, they shrink to zero. Kaneko (1984e) analysed the supercritical behaviour (K>KcK > K_c) of disordered orbits in detail. The universality of this transition was analysed independently by Feigenbaum, Kadanoff, and Shenker (Feigenbaum et al. 1982) and by Rand et al. (1982; Ostlund et al. 1983).

For the specific scan at Ω=0.25\Omega = 0.25, the dominant accumulation is the 1/51/5 locking. Kaneko found that as KK increases toward K∞≈0.15671685K_\infty \approx 0.15671685 (Feigenbaum et al. 1982), the staircase passes through the sequence 2/92/9, 3/143/14, 4/194/19, …\ldots, with general term n/(5n−1)n/(5n-1), before the 1/51/5 plateau opens fully (Kaneko 1982, 1983b). Between successive members of this sequence lie narrower Farey-descendant windows, so the local structure still reflects the standard devil’s-staircase hierarchy.

Diagnostic spotlight: rotation number and Lyapunov exponent

Figure 3 shows the staircase computed with 2×1052 \times 10^5 parameter values (compared to Kaneko’s ∼102\sim 10^2), together with the Lyapunov exponent λ(θn)\lambda(\theta_n) in a separate diagnostic panel. The rotation-number plot isolates the period-adding sequence approaching the 1/51/5 locking, while the Lyapunov panel separates the invertible quasi-periodic regime (λ≈0\lambda\approx 0), the stable locked windows (λ<0\lambda< 0), and the chaotic regime that appears after the 1/51/5 orbit loses stability.

The Lyapunov exponent is the natural diagnostic for the circle map: it directly distinguishes locked motion (λ<0\lambda< 0) from quasi-periodic motion (λ=0\lambda= 0) and chaos (λ>0\lambda> 0), providing a one-number summary of the dynamics at each parameter value.

The self-similar locking hierarchy becomes clearer when the staircase is resolved near the onset of the 1/51/5 plateau. Figure 4 zooms into the K∈[0.125,0.158]K \in [0.125, 0.158] region containing the dominant period-adding sequence 2/9→3/14→4/19→⋯→1/52/9 \to 3/14 \to 4/19 \to \cdots \to 1/5, computed with 10510^5 parameter values. At this scale the successive rational plateaus converging on 1/51/5 are cleanly separated, and narrower subwindows appear between them according to the Farey-tree construction. The point of the zoom is therefore not a generic “between 1/31/3 and 1/21/2” staircase, but the specific accumulation sequence Kaneko analysed in the Ω=0.25\Omega = 0.25 problem (Kaneko 1982, 1983b).

Amplitude Instability: Oscillation, Doubling, and Fractalization

Torus oscillation entered the literature in “Oscillation and Doubling of Torus” (Kaneko 1984c) as a route to chaos distinct from frequency locking and from period doubling. To see it, one needs a map that carries a smooth invariant circle whose amplitude can go unstable independently of its phase. The delayed logistic map does this with a memory term (yn+1=xny_{n+1} = x_n) in place of explicit time dependence: the torus lives in a two-dimensional state space and its shape can deform continuously. Sweeping the single parameter DD in this one model produces all three fates, oscillation, doubling, and fractalization; the monograph (Kaneko 1986a) treats them together. Here Fig. 1 of (Kaneko 1984c) is recomputed with 100 000 iterates per panel, and a 2000-value Lyapunov sweep, the correlation dimension, and SALI are added.

The delayed logistic map (canonical model)

Kaneko (1984c) studied torus instabilities in their purest form using the two-dimensional delayed logistic map: xn+1=αxn+(1−α)(1−Dyn2),yn+1=xn.\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} x_{n+1} &= \alpha\, x_n + (1 - \alpha)(1 - D\, y_n^2), \\ y_{n+1} &= x_n. \end{aligned} \label{eq:delayed_logistic} \end{equation} Here α∈(0,1)\alpha \in (0, 1) is the delay mixing weight (a convex parameter controlling how much the current value xnx_n carries forward versus the nonlinear driving term) and DD is the drive amplitude. The map has a fixed point x*=y*=(1+4D−1)/(2D)x^* = y^* = (\sqrt{1 + 4D} - 1)/(2D). The Jacobian at the fixed point is J=[α−2(1−α)Dx*10]J = \bigl[\begin{smallmatrix} \alpha & -2(1-\alpha)D\,x^* \\ 1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\bigr] with det⁡J=2(1−α)Dx*\det J = 2(1-\alpha)D\,x^*. A Neimark–Sacker bifurcation occurs when det⁡J=1\det J = 1, giving Dc=3−2α4(1−α)2.\begin{equation} D_c = \frac{3 - 2\alpha}{4(1 - \alpha)^2}. \label{eq:hopf_threshold} \end{equation} At α=0.3\alpha = 0.3, Dc≈1.2245D_c \approx 1.2245. For D>DcD > D_c, the fixed point becomes unstable and a smooth torus (invariant closed curve) appears.

Torus oscillation

As DD increases beyond DcD_c, the torus initially grows smoothly. But at a critical value of DD, the torus begins to oscillate: it develops “sidebands” in its power spectrum, corresponding to a modulation of the quasi-periodic motion. This torus oscillation was a new phenomenon, distinct from both frequency locking and torus doubling.

Kaneko classified the progression as DD increases at α=0.3\alpha = 0.3:

  1. Smooth torus.

  2. Torus beginning to oscillate (sidebands visible).

  3. Frequency locking (periodic orbit inside the torus).

  4. Chaos through torus fractalization.

  5. Developed chaotic attractor.

The Lyapunov spectrum (Figure 7) confirms this progression quantitatively: λ1≈0\lambda_1 \approx 0 in the torus regime and λ1>0\lambda_1 > 0 in the chaotic regime.

The transition from locking to chaos in panels (f)–(h) of Figure 5 compresses a rich micro-structure into three frames. Figure 6 zooms into D∈[1.86,1.95]D \in [1.86, 1.95] with eight closely-spaced DD values, each plotted with 10510^5 iterates. The sequence makes clear that the transition is not a single bifurcation but a compressed interval containing near-locking, relocking, and only then the loss of smooth invariant curves. Beyond D≈1.93D \approx 1.93, the attractor develops fractal filaments before settling into a fully chaotic cloud at the far end of the window.

Torus doubling (Maps I and IV)

Period doubling, the cascade 1→2→4→8→⋯1 \to 2 \to 4 \to 8 \to \cdots whose universality Feigenbaum established (Feigenbaum 1978), is the best-known route to chaos in one-dimensional maps. Kaneko (1983a) showed that a torus can double in the same way.

Just as a fixed point can bifurcate into a period-2 orbit (two alternating points), a smooth invariant circle (𝕋1\mathbb{T}^1 in the map; “torus” in Kaneko’s flow language) can bifurcate into a doubled torus, on which the trajectory alternates between two closed curves on successive iterates. A further doubling produces a 4×4\times-torus, then an 8×8\times-torus, and so on until the tori become so convoluted that they merge into a chaotic attractor.

Kaneko studied four maps exhibiting torus doubling (Kaneko 1983a). Maps (II) and (III) are intermediate cases that interpolate between the three-dimensional Map (I) and the four-dimensional Map (IV); we focus on the two extremes. Map (I) is a three-dimensional delayed logistic: xn+1=αxn+(1−α)(1−Dyn2),yn+1=zn,zn+1=xn,\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} x_{n+1} &= \alpha\, x_n + (1 - \alpha)(1 - D\, y_n^2), \\ y_{n+1} &= z_n, \qquad z_{n+1} = x_n, \end{aligned} \label{eq:map_I} \end{equation} and Map (IV) is a four-dimensional variant: xn+1=αxn+(1−α)(1−Dyn2),yn+1=zn,zn+1=αzn+(1−α)(1−Dwn2),wn+1=xn,\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} x_{n+1} &= \alpha\, x_n + (1 - \alpha)(1 - D\, y_n^2), \\ y_{n+1} &= z_n, \\ z_{n+1} &= \alpha\, z_n + (1 - \alpha)(1 - D\, w_n^2), \\ w_{n+1} &= x_n, \end{aligned} \label{eq:map_IV} \end{equation}

The parameter DD controls how strongly the delayed variable drives the system. As DD increases at fixed α\alpha, the torus undergoes successive doublings: 1×→2×→4×→⋯1\times \to 2\times \to 4\times \to \cdots, accumulating at a critical value D∞D_\infty beyond which the motion is chaotic.

Kaneko found that the doubling cascade obeys a scaling relation analogous to Feigenbaum’s: 2l∝εl−γ,γ≈1/3,\begin{equation} 2^l \propto \varepsilon_l^{-\gamma}, \qquad \gamma \approx 1/3, \label{eq:torus_doubling_scaling} \end{equation} where εl\varepsilon_l is the width of the ll-th doubling tongue. Kaneko reported the exponent γ≈1/3\gamma \approx 1/3 from numerical fits to the first few doublings. We adopt this value rather than refitting it: the cascade is compressed into a narrow parameter window and truncated by the onset of chaos after only a few resolvable doublings, so the evidence we present is the qualitative loop multiplication of the attractor (Fig. 8) rather than an independent exponent. Whether this value is a universal constant, as Feigenbaum’s δ=4.6692…\delta = 4.6692\ldots is for period doubling, is open: no complete renormalisation-group derivation exists.

Fractalization of torus

At the boundary between quasi-periodic motion and chaos, Kaneko (1984b) found no single breaking event but a continuous process: the torus fractalizes.

On a smooth torus, the attractor is a one-dimensional curve (a closed orbit in the Poincaré section). Its fractal dimension is exactly 1. As the control parameter approaches the chaos threshold, the curve develops wrinkles, fine-scale oscillations that fold it at ever smaller length scales. The fractal dimension increases continuously from 1 toward ∼1.3\sim 1.3–1.51.5 at the onset of chaos.

This intermediate object, a fractal invariant set with nonpositive maximal Lyapunov exponent, shares the defining features of a strange nonchaotic attractor (SNA) as formalised in (Grebogi et al. 1984). (Mechanistically, classic SNAs arise in quasiperiodically forced systems; the label “SNA-like” is meant only in the geometric/dynamical sense: fractal geometry with no positive Lyapunov exponent. The qualifier is important because the delayed logistic map in this section is an autonomous two-dimensional map, not the quasiperiodically forced construction in which the classical SNA definition was introduced.)

Diagnostic spotlight: correlation dimension and SALI

We quantify the fractalization using the Grassberger–Procaccia correlation dimension D2D_2 (Grassberger and Procaccia 1983). The correlation integral C(r)=2M(M−1)∑i<jΘ(r−∥𝒙i−𝒙j∥)\begin{equation} C(r) = \frac{2}{M(M-1)} \sum_{i < j} \Theta(r - \|\bm{x}_i - \bm{x}_j\|) \label{eq:correlation_integral} \end{equation} scales as C(r)∼rD2C(r) \sim r^{D_2} in the limit of small rr, where {𝒙i}i=1M\{\bm{x}_i\}_{i=1}^M are MM sampled points on the attractor (after discarding a transient). For a smooth curve, D2=1D_2 = 1; for a fractal set in the plane, 1<D2<21 < D_2 < 2; in general D2D_2 is bounded above by the embedding dimension.

Figure 11 plots D2D_2 versus DD, confirming the growth from D2≈1D_2 \approx 1 in the smooth-torus regime to D2≈1.39±0.007D_2 \approx 1.39 \pm 0.007 once the attractor has clearly lost smoothness. Finite-sample fluctuations and imperfect scaling-window selection produce visible non-monotone dips near the transition, so the curve should be read as a diagnostic trend rather than a strict order parameter.

The SALI (Smaller Alignment Index) (Skokos 2001) provides a complementary alignment diagnostic. It tracks two tangent vectors under the linearised dynamics: for strongly chaotic orbits, both vectors align with the most unstable direction, so SALI=min⁡(∥v̂1+v̂2∥,∥v̂1−v̂2∥)→0\text{SALI} = \min(\|\hat{v}_1 + \hat{v}_2\|, \|\hat{v}_1 - \hat{v}_2\|) \to 0 exponentially. In a two-dimensional map, however, SALI is not an ideal discriminator between invariant-circle motion and weak chaos, because the tangent space is already minimal. We therefore illustrate SALI on the four-dimensional coupled delayed logistic map introduced later in Section 5.1, using it here as a higher-dimensional analogue of the torus-to-chaos problem.

Figure 12 shows SALI time series for four regimes of that coupled delayed system. Because the map is dissipative, the two deviation vectors ultimately align with the single leading direction and SALI decays toward zero in every regime; the decay rate therefore does not cleanly separate regular from chaotic motion here, in contrast to the conservative setting for which the index was originally devised (Skokos 2001).

Symmetry Breaking: Torus Collapse via Coupling

In the same year as the torus-doubling paper, Kaneko took up a second route to chaos, the transition from torus to chaos accompanied by frequency lockings with symmetry breaking (Kaneko 1983c), in two identical logistic maps coupled symmetrically. The coupled logistic map is the simplest system with two-frequency quasi-periodicity built from identical parts, and the x↔yx \leftrightarrow y exchange symmetry gives it a natural order parameter. The result was that symmetry breaking and basin fractality arise from the topology of coexisting attractors and not from parameter-space bifurcations alone; Takesue and Kaneko (1984) then showed that the basin boundaries are self-similar fractals. We pair a coarse (a,ε)(a,\varepsilon) survey with an attractor sequence at fixed ε\varepsilon; this is not a one-to-one reproduction of Kaneko’s fixed-DD regime diagram. The 0-1 test is added as a model-free chaos diagnostic.

The coupled logistic map (canonical model)

To study two-frequency quasi-periodicity (an invariant circle 𝕋1\mathbb{T}^1 in this 2D return map), Kaneko introduced the coupled logistic map (Kaneko 1983c): xn+1=1−axn2+ε(yn−xn),yn+1=1−ayn2+ε(xn−yn),\begin{equation} \begin{aligned} x_{n+1} &= 1 - a\, x_n^2 + \varepsilon\,(y_n - x_n), \\ y_{n+1} &= 1 - a\, y_n^2 + \varepsilon\,(x_n - y_n), \end{aligned} \label{eq:coupled_logistic} \end{equation} where aa controls the nonlinearity (logistic map parameter) and ε\varepsilon is the coupling strength. This map has a symmetry x↔yx \leftrightarrow y; when ε≠0\varepsilon\neq 0 and aa is not too large, this symmetry supports a quasi-periodic attractor (torus).

High-Dimensional Extension: Three-Torus and Forced Phase Maps

“Fates of Three-Torus” (Kaneko 1984a) addresses the Ruelle–Takens conjecture that three-frequency quasi-periodicity is structurally unstable. Ruelle and Takens (1971) had argued that 𝕋3\mathbb{T}^3 is generically destroyed by perturbations, and Grebogi, Ott, and Yorke (Grebogi et al. 1983) had found numerically that three-frequency orbits are rare. Kaneko’s question was what replaces the torus. A four-dimensional coupled delayed logistic map removes every continuous-time complication and leaves the competition among three frequencies in the simplest discrete setting. In that map he found a double devil’s staircase of lockings, a new fractalization phenomenon, and a transition to chaos that passes through intermediate lockings (𝕋3→𝕋2→\mathbb{T}^3 \to \mathbb{T}^2 \to cycle →\to chaos) and never, in his scans, leaves 𝕋3\mathbb{T}^3 directly. This is a longer list than the stable-or-unstable verdict of Ruelle–Takens. Grebogi et al. (1985) independently studied attractors on NN-tori. We recompute the double staircase and representative (xn,zn)(x_n, z_n) projections at modern resolution.

The mechanisms in Sections 2–4 act in one or two dimensions. The next step adds a third incommensurate frequency.

The coupled delayed logistic map

A three-torus 𝕋3\mathbb{T}^3 is a quasi-periodic attractor with three independent, incommensurate frequencies ω1,ω2,ω3\omega_1, \omega_2, \omega_3. It is the dynamical object that appears in Landau’s route to turbulence (Landau 1944) after three Hopf bifurcations. Ruelle and Takens (1971) argued that 𝕋3\mathbb{T}^3 should be structurally unstable: generic perturbations destroy it and replace it with a strange attractor. Newhouse et al. Diagnostic spotlight: permutation entropy and complexity-entropy plane

Permutation entropy (Bandt and Pompe 2002) quantifies the complexity of a time series by examining the relative ordering of dd consecutive values. Each window of dd values defines an ordinal pattern (permutation of {0,1,…,d−1}\{0, 1, \ldots, d-1\}). The normalised Shannon entropy of the pattern distribution gives HPE=−1log⁡(d!)∑πpπlog⁡pπ∈[0,1].\begin{equation} H_\text{PE} = -\frac{1}{\log(d!)} \sum_\pi p_\pi \log p_\pi \in [0, 1]. \end{equation}

Periodic orbits produce only a few ordinal patterns (HPE≈0H_\text{PE} \approx 0); more irregular dynamics produces many, pushing HPEH_\text{PE} upward. Combined with the Jensen–Shannon statistical complexity CJSC_\text{JS} (Rosso et al. 2007), the pair (HPE,CJS)(H_\text{PE}, C_\text{JS}) locates the dynamics in a complexity-entropy plane (Figure 22). Noise occupies the high-HH, low-CC corner; chaos occupies an intermediate region; and periodic motion clusters near the origin.

Spatial Extension: Coupled Map Lattices

The coupled map lattice (Kaneko 1985) followed Kaneko’s study of kink–antikink dynamics (Kaneko 1984d). It is the simplest spatially extended dynamical system one can write: discrete time, discrete space, continuous state, and no partial differential equation. The question it was built for is what changes when many chaotic maps are coupled in space. The answer that mattered most was spatiotemporal intermittency (STI), a coexistence of laminar and bursting regions whose scaling has been conjectured to fall in the directed-percolation universality class (Pomeau 1986). In the coupled-map-lattice and globally-coupled-map sections, “turbulent” and “turbulence” mean spatiotemporally disordered, high-dimensional chaotic phases in Kaneko’s sense, not solutions of the Navier–Stokes equations. Waller and Kapral (1984) and Kapral (1985) introduced similar lattices of coupled oscillators independently, and Chaté and Manneville (Chaté and Manneville 1988a) later studied the STI transition in detail. We recompute Kaneko’s spacetime diagrams on 200 sites for 500 steps, add a variance-based activity map on a 200 by 160 parameter grid, and use recurrence quantification analysis as a single-site benchmark.

CML definition and three models

A coupled map lattice (CML) is a discrete-time, discrete-space dynamical system (Kaneko 1985): xn+1(i)=f(xn(i))+ε2[g(xn(i+1))+g(xn(i−1))−2g(xn(i))],\begin{equation} x_{n+1}(i) = f\bigl(x_n(i)\bigr) + \frac{\varepsilon}{2}\Bigl[g\bigl(x_n(i+1)\bigr) + g\bigl(x_n(i-1)\bigr) - 2\, g\bigl(x_n(i)\bigr)\Bigr], \label{eq:cml} \end{equation} where i=1,…,Ni = 1, \ldots, N are lattice sites with periodic boundary conditions, f(x)f(x) is the local map, g(x)g(x) is the coupling function, and ε\varepsilon is the coupling strength. The diffusive coupling on the right-hand side is the discrete Laplacian Δg=gi+1+gi−1−2gi\Delta g = g_{i+1} + g_{i-1} - 2g_i, the simplest model of nearest-neighbour interaction.

Kaneko studied three CML models with different local dynamics:

  1. Piecewise linear map with g=fg = f.

  2. Coupled circle maps with f(θ)=θ+Ksin⁡(2πθ)+Ωf(\theta) = \theta + K\sin(2\pi\theta) + \Omega and g(θ)=sin⁡(2πθ)g(\theta) = \sin(2\pi\theta).

  3. Coupled logistic maps with f(x)=1−ax2f(x) = 1 - ax^2 and g=fg = f.

Spatiotemporal intermittency and directed percolation

All three models exhibit spatiotemporal intermittency (STI): the spacetime diagram (Figure 23) shows alternating laminar (ordered) and burst (disordered) regions. As the coupling ε\varepsilon is tuned, the fraction of bursts changes continuously, analogous to a phase transition.

Kaneko reduced the laminar/burst dynamics to a binary (cellular automaton) description (Kaneko 1985) and characterised the transition through burst-propagation speed vB∝(ε−εc)νv_B \propto (\varepsilon- \varepsilon_c)^\nu with ν≈0.6\nu \approx 0.6–0.90.9, and the growth of positive Lyapunov exponents. Pomeau (1986) suggested a connection to the universality class of directed percolation (DP), the standard absorbing-state transition of non-equilibrium statistical mechanics (Hinrichsen 2000). Chaté and Manneville (Chaté and Manneville 1988a, 1988b) investigated this connection and found DP-like scaling features in deterministic spatiotemporal intermittency, while noting that the critical exponents may depend on whether the laminar state is strictly absorbing (Chaté and Manneville 1989b, 1989a); Kaneko himself noted that “the universality class on critical exponents seems to be different” (Kaneko 1989b).

To complement the qualitative spacetime view with quantitative measures, we extract an STI “spine” from the Kaneko Model-A CML (Figure 24): the turbulent fraction ρ\rho as a function of the coupling ε\varepsilon, together with the laminar-domain size distribution. The turbulent fraction has a sharp onset: ρ\rho is near zero below ε≃0.06\varepsilon\simeq 0.06 and then rises steeply to a burst-dominated plateau with ρ\rho near 0.880.88. The laminar cluster-size distribution shows an approximately exponential tail (decay rate ≈2.11\approx 2.11 over the accessible range), consistent with DP-like phenomenology; the universality class itself is left open, for the reasons above.

Pattern dynamics (five phases)

Kaneko (1989b) systematically classified the dynamics of the CML [eq:cml] with the logistic map f(x)=1−ax2f(x) = 1 - ax^2 and g=fg = f. The canonical taxonomy in the (a,ε)(a, \varepsilon) plane contains five phases:

  1. Frozen random (a≲1.5a \lesssim 1.5, moderate ε\varepsilon): the lattice settles into a static, spatially disordered pattern.

  2. Pattern selection (a≈1.6a \approx 1.6–1.71.7): the lattice spontaneously selects a spatially periodic pattern, breaking translational symmetry.

  3. Defect turbulence (a≈1.7a \approx 1.7–1.81.8): the selected pattern is disrupted by defects (domain walls) that nucleate, propagate, and annihilate chaotically.

  4. Pattern competition intermittency (a≈1.8a \approx 1.8–1.91.9): large coherent domains compete for territory, punctuated by intermittent bursts of turbulence.

  5. Fully developed turbulence (a≳1.9a \gtrsim 1.9): all spatial coherence is lost.

Co-moving Lyapunov exponent and information flow

Kaneko (1986b) introduced the co-moving Lyapunov exponent λ(v)\lambda(v) to quantify how perturbations propagate through a CML. Thermodynamics of spatiotemporal chaos

For a CML with NN sites, the full Lyapunov spectrum {λi}i=1N\{\lambda_i\}_{i=1}^{N} grows with system size. Kaneko (1989c) introduced the concept of intensive thermodynamic quantities for spatiotemporal chaos: the KS entropy density hKS=HKS/Nh_\text{KS} = H_\text{KS}/N, the Lyapunov dimension density dKY/Nd_\text{KY}/N, and the density of positive Lyapunov exponents. When the spatial correlation length ξ\xi is much smaller than the system size NN, these densities converge to well-defined intensive values, and the CML behaves like a thermodynamic system with local degrees of freedom.

This convergence breaks down near phase transitions (where ξ→∞\xi \to \infty) and in the pattern selection phase (where long-range spatial order imposes correlations across the lattice). The curves are consistent with short, few-site correlation lengths together with slower finite-size convergence in the frozen and pattern-selection cases, but they should not be read as a rigorous thermodynamic proof on their own.

Diagnostic spotlight: RQA and spacetime recurrence

Recurrence plots (Eckmann et al. 1987; Marwan et al. 2007) visualise the times at which a trajectory returns to a previously visited neighbourhood.   • RR (recurrence rate): fraction of recurrent points.

  • DET (determinism): fraction in diagonal lines (≥lmin\ge l_\text{min}).

  • LAM (laminarity): fraction in vertical lines (≥vmin\ge v_\text{min}).

RQA is a natural diagnostic for the laminar/burst dichotomy that defines STI: the laminarity measure LAM tracks the fraction of near-recurrent (laminar) states, while ENTR captures the structural complexity of the laminar/burst boundaries.

Diagnostic spotlight: the temporal-intermittency taxonomy

The laminar/burst dichotomy that organises spatiotemporal intermittency has a classical low-dimensional counterpart. Pomeau and Manneville (1980) classified the intermittent routes to chaos by the way a periodic orbit loses stability: Type I through a tangent (saddle–node) bifurcation, Type II through a subcritical Hopf bifurcation, and Type III through a subharmonic (inverse period-doubling) bifurcation. A distinct mechanism, on–off intermittency, arises when an invariant subspace loses transverse stability at a blowout bifurcation (Platt et al. 1993; Heagy et al. 1994). We apply laminar-phase detection and heavy-tail estimators (maximum-likelihood exponent with bootstrap confidence interval and a goodness-of-fit pp-value) to canonical realisations of each route, as a check that the diagnostics behave correctly where the underlying scaling laws are known.

These temporal routes give the spatiotemporal case a vocabulary, not a decomposition. Each laminar interval in the coupled-map-lattice intermittency of Section 6.2 is, locally in time, a site lingering near a marginally stable state before a burst, and the classical types list the ways such local stability can be lost. No single type accounts for the full lattice dynamics, which couple these local events across space.

Mean-Field Extension: Globally Coupled Maps

The globally coupled map (Kaneko 1990b, 1989a) replaces the nearest-neighbour diffusion of the CML with all-to-all coupling, its mean-field limit. With no spatial structure left, what remains is the relation between chaos in each element and order in the whole, and the question Kaneko put to it was whether a system can be chaotic element by element and still fail to self-average. Three phenomena came out of it that a nearest-neighbour lattice cannot show: dynamical clustering (symmetry breaking among identical elements), a failure of the law of large numbers (the mean field keeps finite fluctuations as N→∞N \to \infty), and collective chaos (the mean field has its own low-dimensional chaotic dynamics). Clustering was developed further in (Kaneko 1990a, 1991, 1992a). We recompute Kaneko’s MSD-versus-NN plot (Fig. 1 of (Kaneko 1990b)) and add figures of cluster states and of the collective Lyapunov exponent.

Kaneko (1990b) introduced the globally coupled map (GCM): xn+1(i)=(1−ε)f(xn(i))+εN∑j=1Nf(xn(j)),\begin{equation} x_{n+1}(i) = (1 - \varepsilon)\, f\bigl(x_n(i)\bigr) + \frac{\varepsilon}{N} \sum_{j=1}^{N} f\bigl(x_n(j)\bigr), \label{eq:gcm} \end{equation} where the mean field hn=N−1∑jf(xn(j))h_n = N^{-1} \sum_j f(x_n(j)) couples every site to every other.

Violation of the law of large numbers

Kaneko’s claim is that collective chaos violates the law of large numbers but not the central limit theorem: P(h)P(h) approaches Gaussian form, yet its variance stays macroscopic (Kaneko 1990b).

For NN independent, identically distributed random variables, the variance of the mean decreases as 1/N1/N. Kaneko showed that for the GCM at certain parameter values, the mean-square deviation (MSD) MSD=⟨(δh)2⟩=⟨(hn−⟨hn⟩)2⟩\begin{equation} \text{MSD} = \langle (\delta h)^2 \rangle = \langle (h_n - \langle h_n \rangle)^2 \rangle \end{equation} of the mean field hnh_n does not decrease as 1/N1/N. Instead, it saturates at a finite value as N→∞N \to \infty (Figure 34). We compute the MSD for N=100N = 100 to 20,00020{,}000 at five values of aa, with 5×1045 \times 10^4 time steps after a 5×1035 \times 10^3-step transient.

This “violation of the law of large numbers” (more precisely, a failure of the usual 1/N1/N scaling) is a manifestation of collective chaos (Shibata and Kaneko 1998): the mean field itself undergoes low-dimensional chaotic motion, decoupled from the high-dimensional dynamics of individual sites. With increasing NN, the distribution P(h)P(h) of the mean field (Figure 35) approaches an approximately Gaussian form, yet its variance stops decreasing beyond a crossover size, so the law of large numbers fails while CLT-like Gaussianity can still hold (Kaneko 1990b, 1992a).

Collective chaos and cluster states

The nontrivial mean-field dynamics indicates that the NN oscillators, although not synchronised, maintain correlations that resist averaging. Kaneko showed that the system self-organises into cluster states: groups of oscillators that share the same trajectory, with the number and sizes of clusters depending on aa and ε\varepsilon. These clusters are not imposed by the coupling topology (which is all-to-all and thus featureless) but emerge from the dynamics, a form of dynamical symmetry breaking.

Clustered attractors and switching

In the GCM, a cluster state is a partition of the NN sites into kk groups with nearly identical instantaneous values at a given iterate. The partition is characterised by the cluster multiplicities (N1,N2,…,Nk)(N_1, N_2, \ldots, N_k) with ∑lNl=N\sum_l N_l = N. Kaneko (1990a) showed that at moderate coupling (ε≈0.1\varepsilon\approx 0.1), the GCM spontaneously selects a small number of clusters (k=2k = 2–55), and that the partition coding provides a discrete “label” for each macroscopic state.

Kaneko also found posi-nega switching (Kaneko 1989a): the system can hop between different clustered attractors under controlled perturbations that change the cluster size partition. This switching is not random noise but follows deterministic rules governed by the basin structure of the clustered attractors (Kaneko 1991). Figure 36 visualises the cluster structure as a spacetime diagram after sorting the site values at each time step, so that coherent clusters appear as broad plateaus whose widths encode their populations.

Collective degrees of freedom

Shibata and Kaneko (1998) showed that the mean field of the GCM can have a positive collective Lyapunov exponent λc>0\lambda_c > 0: a small perturbation to the macroscopic mean field hn=N−1∑jf(xn(j))h_n = N^{-1}\sum_j f(x_n(j)) grows exponentially, even though the microscopic dynamics of individual sites may be independently chaotic with very different exponents. This defines a collective chaos phase in which the macroscopic observable has its own low-dimensional chaotic dynamics, distinct from the high-dimensional microscopic chaos.

The partially ordered (PO) phase, between full synchronisation and full turbulence, is characterised by clusters that form, merge, and split in a marginally stable regime. Kaneko (1994a) showed that in this regime, information cascades through the cluster hierarchy with marginal stability: the system sits at the edge of a bifurcation, generating long-range temporal correlations. Takeuchi, Ginelli, and Chaté (Takeuchi et al. 2009) later showed that co-moving Lyapunov analysis in large CML systems helps quantify collective dynamics. Figure 37 plots a finite-time estimate of the collective Lyapunov exponent as a function of aa.

Impact within the 1990s

Two lines of work in the 1990s followed directly from the GCM. Dynamic clustering provided a framework for understanding cell differentiation in biological networks (Kaneko 1994b, 1997a): the spontaneous partition of identical coupled elements into distinct groups mirrors the emergence of cell types from genetically identical precursors. Milnor attractor dominance (Kaneko 1997b, 1998), in which noise-induced transitions between coexisting attractors are governed by the Milnor attractor structure and not by basin volume, connected the GCM to stochastic dynamics and ergodic theory.

Glossary

Definitions of the terms used throughout the paper.

Arnold tongue

A wedge-shaped region in the two-parameter plane of a periodically forced oscillator within which the oscillator frequency locks to a rational multiple of the driving frequency. Named after V. I. Arnol’d.

Collective chaos

Chaotic dynamics of the macroscopic mean field in a system of many coupled elements, persisting even as the number of elements N→∞N \to \infty.

Devil’s staircase

A monotone function (typically rotation number versus a parameter) consisting of infinitely many rational plateaus (lockings) interspersed with a Cantor set of irrational values.

Directed percolation (DP)

A non-equilibrium phase transition universality class describing the spreading of an “active” state into an “absorbing” state on a lattice.

Farey tree

A hierarchical organisation of rational numbers in which the mediant (p+p′)/(q+q′)(p+p')/(q+q') of two fractions p/qp/q and p′/q′p'/q' appears between them.

Frequency locking

Synchronisation of an oscillator to a rational frequency ratio with an external drive or a coupled partner; the quasi-periodic orbit degenerates into a periodic one.

Lyapunov exponent

The rate of exponential divergence (or convergence) of nearby trajectories. Positive: chaos. Zero: quasi-periodic (marginal) direction. Negative: stable direction.

Neimark–Sacker bifurcation

The discrete-map analogue of a Hopf bifurcation: a fixed point loses stability and an invariant closed curve (torus) is born.

Rotation number

The average angle advanced per iteration on a circle map or invariant circle, ρ=lim⁡n→∞(θn−θ0)/n\rho = \lim_{n\to\infty} (\theta_n - \theta_0)/n.

Strange nonchaotic attractor (SNA)

An attractor with fractal geometry (non-integer dimension) but no positive Lyapunov exponent: geometrically strange, dynamically regular.

Discussion

Kaneko’s papers of 1982–1998 established the vocabulary and the phenomenology of torus dynamics and spatiotemporal chaos. Ordered by the six mechanisms of the Introduction, from phase instability to mean-field coupling, they read as a sequence of steps in complexity instead of a chronology.

This ordering matters because the same operation recurs across different models: add one structural degree of freedom, then ask which invariant object loses stability and which observable records the loss. In the circle map the relevant observable is a rotation number; in torus oscillation and doubling it is the Lyapunov spectrum and the geometry of an invariant circle; in the CML it is the propagation of perturbations through space; in the GCM it is the persistence of mean-field fluctuations as NN grows. The atlas complements broad CML metric surveys such as Nolan et al. (2026), which sample many systems and metrics but do not retrace Kaneko’s sequence of mechanisms and figures.

Mechanistic interpretation

The mechanism-based ordering also explains why the same diagnostic cannot carry the whole paper. The appropriate observable is fixed by the invariant object being destabilised. In the phase-locking sections, that object is an invariant circle with a well-defined winding rate, so the rotation number is the natural observable. For torus oscillation and doubling, the change is not only in frequency content but in the stability of transverse directions, which makes the Lyapunov spectrum decisive. The same logic fixes the remaining pairings: basin and order-parameter views for symmetry breaking, where exchange-related attractors coexist; spatial diagnostics for CMLs, where perturbations propagate through laminar and burst regions; and finite-size and mean-field statistics for GCMs, where persistent correlations among nominally identical elements survive averaging.

Higher resolution makes these distinctions visible. Narrow windows resolve locking transitions organised by rational approximants, long trajectories reduce misclassification of weak chaos and nearly neutral tori, and finite-size sweeps test whether collective chaos persists as mean-field averaging becomes more effective. A diagnostic is decisive only when the degree of freedom that goes unstable is one it can see.

Toric chaos

Yamagishi and Kaneko (2020) showed that chaos can exist on a high-dimensional torus and not only through its collapse. In systems of globally coupled phase oscillators, the attractor can be a high-dimensional torus 𝕋k\mathbb{T}^k (with k≫1k \gg 1) on which the dynamics is chaotic, which they call “toric chaos.” This adds a fate to Kaneko’s list for 𝕋3\mathbb{T}^3 and beyond: a higher-dimensional torus can carry chaos without collapsing.

The coupled delayed logistic map (Section 5.1) supports three-torus motion that collapses to chaos as DBD_B increases at fixed coupling (Figure 17). At the transition, two Lyapunov exponents are near zero while one becomes positive. This is the classical torus collapse, not toric chaos in the sense of Yamagishi and Kaneko (2020), which requires the torus to persist as a manifold even while the on-torus dynamics is chaotic.

Biological relevance

Kaneko extended the dynamical clustering framework to biological networks. Kaneko (1994b) showed that the partition structure of GCM cluster states provides a natural encoding of cell-type diversity. In coupled maps with growth and death dynamics, clusters of oscillators that share a common time trace can then be reinterpreted as cell types, with cluster formation playing the role of cell differentiation (Kaneko 1997a). Ecological applications of coupled logistic maps appeared early (Satoh and Aihara 1990; Satoh 1991). In a recent preprint, Shimada and Kaneko (2026) continue this direction by connecting high-dimensional chaos and intermittent switching to coexistence in cross-feeding microbial-community models. In these models the macroscopic state is carried by partitions that form dynamically, not by fixed species labels or imposed network modules, which is why the mean-field and clustering mechanisms are still in use.

Diagnostic synergies

The four modern diagnostics distributed across the preceding sections provide complementary views of Kaneko’s transitions:

  • The 0-1 test (Section 4) is the most practical for binary classification (chaos vs. regular) from scalar data because it does not require equations or tangent maps.

  • SALI (Section 3) needs the Jacobian, and in the dissipative maps used here its decay rate did not separate regular from chaotic motion; its clean dichotomy is established for conservative systems.

  • Permutation entropy (Section 5) works from the ordinal patterns of a scalar observable alone, so it tracks regular windows and irregular bands without a Jacobian.

  • RQA (Section 6) is best for detecting intermittency and laminar/burst transitions because vertical and diagonal recurrence structures respond directly to laminar residence times and repeated local patterns.

These diagnostics are not interchangeable. Each responds to a different way in which order is lost, and the failure of SALI on the dissipative maps above is one reason a single diagnostic cannot carry the paper.

Computational limitations: then and now

Kaneko’s original parameter scans typically used ∼100\sim 100 points with ∼50,000\sim 50{,}000 iterations per point. Our scans use from 500 (Figure 17) to 200 000 (Figure 3) parameter values (Table 4). This increase matters because several transitions are organised by narrow windows, thin strands, or fractal boundaries; at the original resolution these structures can be inferred from theory but not inspected visually. The fine structure of Arnold tongue boundaries, the strand separation in torus doubling, and the fractal detail at basin boundaries therefore become part of the evidence rather than background expectation.

What persisted after the 1990s

Three later lines of work extended the framework beyond spatiotemporal chaos. First, the collective chaos discovered in the GCM led to a systematic study of Milnor attractor networks (Kaneko 1997b, 1998): in systems with many coexisting attractors, noise-induced transitions are governed by the Milnor attractor structure and not by basin volume. Second, chaotic itinerancy, a slow, directed wandering among partially stable attractor ruins, became an organising idea for high-dimensional chaos (Kaneko 1992b, 1995). Third, the dynamic clustering mechanism was reinterpreted as a model of cell differentiation: coupled maps with growth and death dynamics produce spontaneous diversification of cell types (Kaneko 1997a). Separately, Nishikawa and Kaneko (Nishikawa and Kaneko 1996) connected fractalized tori to strange nonchaotic attractors (SNAs).

Open questions

Several questions raised by Kaneko’s work remain open:

  1. Is the torus-doubling exponent γ≈1/3\gamma \approx 1/3 truly universal? Kaneko’s numerical evidence is suggestive but not definitive. Partial renormalisation-group results exist for the torus-doubling terminal point and for quasi-periodically forced one-dimensional maps (Kuznetsov et al. 1998; Jorba et al. 2015, 2016), but a complete renormalisation-group analysis analogous to Feigenbaum’s for period doubling has not been achieved. A fixed point of the doubling operator, with its eigenvalue compared against the fitted value, would settle it.

  2. What is the complete classification of 𝕋3\mathbb{T}^3 fates? Kaneko identified locking, fractalization, and chaos, but the measure-theoretic structure of the phase diagram (which fates are generic, which are exceptional) is not fully understood; the exponential rarity of high-dimensional tori found by Yamagishi and Kaneko (2020) is partial progress. A measure of the locked, fractalized, and chaotic regions as the grid is refined, with a convergence test, would say which fates are generic.

  3. Can the directed percolation universality of STI be proven rigorously? The numerical evidence is suggestive, the exponents are disputed (Section 6.2), and no proof connects deterministic CML dynamics to the DP class. Finite-size scaling of the turbulent fraction on lattices of 256 to 4096 sites, with the exponents compared against DP within error bars, would settle the numerical side; a proof is a separate matter.

  4. Does chaos sustain biological diversity? Kaneko’s clustering models of cell differentiation (Kaneko 1994b, 1997a) and ecological applications (Satoh and Aihara 1990; Satoh 1991) connect the two fields. Shimada and Kaneko (Shimada and Kaneko 2026) ask a concrete form of the question: whether the number of coexisting species exceeds the competitive-exclusion bound in the high-dimensional chaotic regime. The test is a count of coexisting species against that bound in the same model with and without chaos.

Conclusions

Kaneko’s papers of 1982–1998 have been recomputed and arranged by mechanism. Each of the six chapters adds one structural degree of freedom to the one before.

Organising the work this way makes a recurring construction easy to see: a low-dimensional invariant object is destabilised, then a new degree of freedom (amplitude, symmetry, spatial propagation, or mean-field correlation) becomes dynamically active. This pattern was already present in Kaneko’s original papers; recomputing the figures at higher resolution and viewing the models side by side brings it into a single view.

The principal contributions of this work are:

  1. Kaneko’s results from nine papers are arranged by physical mechanism (Table 2).

  2. Selected figures are recomputed at finer parameter resolution, which resolves structure the original scans could only infer.

  3. Four modern diagnostics are applied as spotlights inside the mechanism chapters.

  4. One notation (Table 1) replaces the parameter symbols that varied across Kaneko’s papers and separates the drive parameters (DAD_A, DBD_B) from the correlation dimension D2D_2.

  5. The map-versus-flow convention for counting Lyapunov exponents on kk-tori is stated once and used throughout (Section 1).

The atlas is a reference point: old and new computations of the same models can be compared directly. It establishes no new scaling law, does not replace finite-size scaling for STI, and does not turn the diagnostic spotlights into validated automatic classifiers. Next steps are a controlled finite-size-scaling study of the STI examples, an uncertainty estimate for the torus-doubling exponent, and a comparison between the spotlights used here and larger metric suites for CMLs.

Assumptions and Validity Checklist

  • Discrete-time maps: all models are nondimensional iterated maps; “time” is iteration count nn.

  • Rotation numbers: computed via the lift (unwrapped phase); near-critical convergence is verified by doubling the averaging length.

  • Lyapunov spectra: computed via QR/Gram–Schmidt re-orthonormalization after a discarded transient; reported curves are stable under doubling the averaging length.

  • CML boundary conditions: periodic in space unless stated; diagnostics sampling along a ray (e.g. λ(v)\lambda(v)) use windows short enough to avoid wrap-around artifacts (see Sec. 6.4).

  • Fractal dimension estimates: correlation dimension D2D_2 depends on finite sampling (MM points), temporal correlations (mitigated by the Theiler window (Theiler 1986)), and scaling-region selection; the ±1σ\pm1\sigma fit uncertainty on D2D_2 is reported in Figure 11.

  • Universality-class language (e.g. “DP-like”): treated as conjectural unless supported by finite-size scaling with uncertainty estimates.
